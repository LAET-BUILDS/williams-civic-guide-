California Article XIII A: Proposition 13, the 1% cap and two-thirds votes

Lesson 21 of 68 · Taxes, budgets, fees and utilities · 11 min read

Article XIII A is Proposition 13, adopted by initiative on June 6, 1978 Verified1. It caps property tax and makes new taxes harder to pass. Our reading: it is a main reason California cities lean on sales taxes, fees and charges rather than property tax.

Key takeaways

  • Property tax can't exceed "One percent (1%) of the full cash value" (§1(a)), plus voter-approved debt (§1(b)).
  • Value resets at purchase, new construction or change in ownership; otherwise it rises by inflation "not to exceed 2 percent" a year (§2).
  • New or higher state taxes need two-thirds of each house (§3).
  • Local special taxes need "a two-thirds vote of the qualified electors" (§4). Article XIII C later added rules for general taxes.

Section 1: The 1% cap

Exceptions. The cap doesn't apply to taxes paying for "Indebtedness approved by the voters prior to July 1, 1978," bonds for real property approved "by two-thirds of the votes cast," and school bonds approved by "55 percent of the voters" with accountability requirements (§1(b)) Verified1.

Section 2: Value and the 2% cap

Section 2 is over 17,000 characters. Read it in full on our California Constitution pages. The core:

"The 'full cash value' means the county assessor's valuation of real property as shown on the 1975–76 tax bill under 'full cash value' or, thereafter, the appraised value of real property when purchased, newly constructed, or a change in ownership has occurred after the 1975 assessment." (§2(a)) Verified1

"The full cash value base may reflect from year to year the inflationary rate not to exceed 2 percent for any given year or reduction as shown in the consumer price index or comparable data for the area under taxing jurisdiction" (§2(b)) Verified1

§2 also contains disaster and seismic-retrofit rules, transfers by older and disabled homeowners, and Section 2.1's family-property rules Verified1. Subdivision (h) became "inoperative February 16, 2021, pursuant to Section 2.1 of this article" Verified1.

Section 3: State taxes

Section 3's definition of "tax" parallels the local definition in Article XIII C, §1(e) Verified1.

Section 4: Local special taxes

"Special" means earmarked. The Supreme Court read "special taxes" to cover "taxes earmarked for a specific purpose" (City and County of San Francisco v. Farrell (1982) 32 Cal.3d 47) Verified2. In Richmond (1982), a transportation sales tax adopted by majority vote stood: "We hold that the sales tax in issue here was validly adopted by a majority vote" (Los Angeles County Transportation Com. v. Richmond (1982) 31 Cal.3d 197) Verified3. Voters closed many of those gaps in 1996 with Article XIII C, which sorts every local tax into general or special Verified1.

The constitutional challenge

Opponents argued Proposition 13 was too sweeping to be adopted by initiative. The Court disagreed: "We conclude that article XIII A fairly may be deemed a constitutional amendment, not a revision" (Amador Valley Joint Union High Sch. Dist. v. State Bd. of Equalization (1978) 22 Cal.3d 208) Verified4. The U.S. Supreme Court later rejected an equal protection challenge to acquisition-value assessment (Nordlinger v. Hahn, 505 U.S. 1 (1992)) Verified5.

In Williams

  • Your tax bill reflects the 1% cap plus any voter-approved debt Verified1. Colusa County collects and apportions it ("The one percent (1%) tax to be collected by the counties") Verified1.
  • A new Williams special tax (for example, a parcel tax for a named service) needs two-thirds voter approval (§4; XIII C §2(d)) Verified1.
  • General law cities can levy charter-city taxes "subject to the voters' approval pursuant to Article XIIIA" (Gov. Code §37100.5) Verified6.

Common misconceptions

  • "Prop 13 freezes taxes." No. Assessed value can rise up to 2% a year and resets on sale or new construction (§2) Verified1.
  • "Prop 13 only affects homes." It applies to "real property" generally Verified1.

Check yourself

Answer in your head first, then open each question.

What is the maximum ad valorem property tax rate under §1(a)?

One percent of full cash value Verified1.

How much can assessed value rise in a year without a sale or new construction?

Up to 2 percent, tracking inflation (§2(b)) Verified1.

What vote does a local special tax need?

Two-thirds of qualified electors (§4) Verified1.

Was Proposition 13 an invalid revision?

No. Amador held it "fairly may be deemed a constitutional amendment, not a revision" Verified4.
