California Article I (part 1): Liberty, privacy and equality

Lesson 40 of 68 · California Constitution: overview and Article I · 17 min read

Article I is California's Declaration of Rights. It's longer than the federal Bill of Rights and often protects more. This lesson covers its liberty and equality sections. Speech and religion, criminal justice, and property are in the next three lessons.

Key takeaways

  • California's privacy right is written into §1 and applies to private businesses as well as government.
  • §7 gives due process and equal protection under state law, which California courts can read more broadly than the Fourteenth Amendment.
  • §7(b) bans special privileges "not granted on the same terms to all citizens."
  • §31 (Proposition 209) bans race and sex preferences in public employment, education and contracting, including by cities.

Why the state rights matter

"Rights guaranteed by this Constitution are not dependent on those guaranteed by the United States Constitution" (§24) Verified1. The U.S. Supreme Court accepts a state's "sovereign right to adopt in its own constitution individual liberties more expansive than those conferred by the Federal Constitution" (PruneYard Shopping Center v. Robins, 447 U.S. 74 (1980)) Verified2. See Criminal justice: §24 for the criminal-case exception.

Section 1: Inalienable rights and privacy

  • Privacy by initiative. The word "privacy" was added "by an initiative adopted by the voters on November 7, 1972" Verified3.
  • Early application. In White v. Davis (1975), police "undercover agents" registered as UCLA students and reported on class discussions. The court held the complaint stated "a prima facie violation of freedom of speech and of assembly as well as of the state constitutional right of privacy" (White v. Davis (1975) 13 Cal.3d 757) Verified4.
  • Private actors too. In Hill v. National Collegiate Athletic Assn. (1994) 7 Cal.4th 1, when the NCAA argued the privacy right couldn't be used against a private organization, the California Supreme Court answered: "We disagree" Verified3. A plaintiff must first identify "a specific, legally protected privacy interest" Verified3.

Section 1.1: Reproductive freedom

Adopted November 8, 2022, effective December 21, 2022 Verified1, after Dobbs held "The Constitution does not confer a right to abortion" Verified5. Our reading: this is the clearest example of California protecting a right the federal Constitution no longer does.

Section 5: The military

California's version of the Third Amendment, plus a ban on a peacetime standing army Verified1. See the Third Amendment.

Section 6: Slavery

Matches the Thirteenth Amendment, including the exception "to punish crime."

Section 7: Due process and equal protection

Subdivision (a) mirrors the Fourteenth Amendment. Most of its length concerns school busing; voters added language limiting state courts to federal equal protection standards on pupil assignment and transportation Verified1.

State due process can protect more. The California Supreme Court held that state due process "must be determined in the context of the individual's due process liberty interest in freedom from arbitrary adjudicative procedures," so even when someone loses a benefit the law doesn't treat as property, they may get a chance to respond (People v. Ramirez (1979) 25 Cal.3d 260) Verified6.

State equal protection can protect more. In Serrano v. Priest (1971), the court held "that the right to an education in our public schools is a fundamental interest which cannot be conditioned on wealth" (Serrano v. Priest (1971) 5 Cal.3d 584) Verified7.

Selective enforcement. A person singled out for prosecution by invidious discrimination has a defense: "such a prosecution must collapse upon the sands of prejudice" (Murgia v. Municipal Court (1975) 15 Cal.3d 286) Verified8. Allegations of "a conscious policy of selective enforcement directed against members or supporters of a particular labor organization" were enough to get discovery Verified8.

Subdivision (b): no special privileges. "A citizen or class of citizens may not be granted privileges or immunities not granted on the same terms to all citizens" Verified1. Our reading: it's California's own text against favoritism in permits, waivers and enforcement.

Section 7.5: Marriage

Added November 5, 2024, replacing the former §7.5, which was repealed the same day Verified1. The former section came from 2008's Proposition 8: "Only marriage between a man and a woman is valid or recognized in California" Verified9. The California Supreme Court had held Proposition 8 a valid amendment, not a revision (Strauss v. Horton (2009) 46 Cal.4th 364) Verified9; Obergefell (2015) later held the Fourteenth Amendment requires states to license same-sex marriages Verified10.

Sections 8–11: Business, contracts, debt, habeas corpus

  • §8 bans disqualifying people from a business or job because of "sex, race, creed, color, or national or ethnic origin."
  • §9 copies the federal bans on bills of attainder, ex post facto laws and impairing contracts (Article I, §10).
  • §10 bans jail for civil debt. Our reading: unpaid city fees and civil penalties can be collected by liens and lawsuits, not by jailing the debtor.
  • §11 limits suspending habeas corpus to rebellion or invasion.

Sections 20–22: Noncitizens, separate property, no property test for voting

§22 means no one needs to own property to vote or hold office in Williams Verified1.

Section 31: No preferences (Proposition 209)

It applies to "any city" (§31(f)) Verified1. San Jose required contractors to reach out to or use minority- and women-owned subcontractors. The court held "the City's Program is unconstitutional because the outreach option affords preferential treatment to MBE/WBE subcontractors on the basis of race or sex" (Hi-Voltage Wire Works, Inc. v. City of San Jose (2000) 24 Cal.4th 537) Verified11.

In Williams

  • City contracts can't use race or sex preferences (§31) Verified1,11.
  • Code enforcement that targets one group or person for improper reasons can be challenged under §7 and Murgia Verified8.
  • Privacy limits what the city can collect and disclose, and §3(b)(3) keeps the public-records right from overriding it. See Speech, religion and open government.
  • Favoritism. §7(b) is a state-law hook against waivers granted to some and not others Verified1.

Common misconceptions

  • "California's equal protection is the same as federal." Often similar, but California courts can and do go further (Serrano) Verified7.
  • "Privacy only limits government." §1 reaches private actors too Verified3.

Check yourself

Answer in your head first, then open each question.

When was privacy added to Article I, §1?

November 7, 1972, by initiative Verified3.

Does §31 apply to the City of Williams?

Yes. "State" includes "any city" (§31(f)) Verified1.

What did Serrano v. Priest hold?

Education is a fundamental interest under state equal protection that can't be conditioned on wealth Verified7.

What does §10 say about unpaid civil debts?

A person may not be imprisoned in a civil action for debt or tort Verified1.
