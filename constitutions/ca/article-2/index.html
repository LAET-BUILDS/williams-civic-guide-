California Article II: Voting, initiative, referendum and recall

Lesson 8 of 68 · Voting and elections · 22 min read

The U.S. Constitution has no initiative, referendum or recall. California's Article II puts all three in the voters' hands and requires the Legislature to extend them to cities.

Key takeaways

  • §1: "All political power is inherent in the people."
  • Every city office, including Williams council seats, is nonpartisan (§6).
  • Initiatives must stick to one subject (§8(d)); courts read that loosely: parts must be "reasonably germane."
  • §11 and §19 guarantee local initiative, referendum and recall; the Elections Code supplies the procedure.
  • Courts "jealously guard" the local initiative power (DeVita).

Section 1: Power is in the people

Our reading: §1 is the theory behind everything else in Article II. It isn't usually enforced alone; the specific powers below are.

Sections 2–4: The right to vote

  • 18 and a citizen. §2(a) mirrors the Twenty-Sixth Amendment's age Verified1. See Amendment 26.
  • Parolees. Since December 16, 2020, the bar applies only while "serving a state or federal prison term" (§4), and the vote is restored "upon the completion of their prison term" (§2(b)) Verified1.
  • "Have that vote counted" (§2.5, adopted November 5, 2002) Verified1.

Sections 5–7: Primaries, nonpartisan offices, secret ballot

In Williams. "All judicial, school, county, and city offices ... shall be nonpartisan" (§6(a)), and no party may "nominate a candidate for nonpartisan office" (§6(b)) Verified1. City council candidates appear without party labels. The top-two primary in §5 covers congressional and state offices, not city offices Verified1.

Section 8: The initiative

  • Signatures. 5% of the last vote for Governor for a statute; 8% for a constitutional amendment (§8(b)) Verified1.
  • One subject. "An initiative measure embracing more than one subject may not be submitted to the electors or have any effect" (§8(d)) Verified1. The test is lenient: a measure passes "if, despite its varied collateral effects, all of its parts are 'reasonably germane' to each other," and to its general purpose (Brosnahan v. Brown (1982) 32 Cal.3d 236, quoting Amador Valley Joint Union High Sch. Dist. v. State Bd. of Equalization) Verified2.
  • Amendment vs. revision. Voters may amend the constitution by initiative but not revise it; Article XIII A (Proposition 13) "fairly may be deemed a constitutional amendment, not a revision" (Amador Valley Joint Union High Sch. Dist. v. State Bd. of Equalization (1978) 22 Cal.3d 208) Verified3. See Article XVIII.
  • Who defends it. When state officials declined to defend Proposition 8, the Court held that "in light of the nature and purpose of the initiative process embodied in article II, section 8" official proponents could assert the state's interest (Perry v. Brown (2011) 52 Cal.4th 1116) Verified4.

Sections 9–10: Referendum and effective dates

  • What's exempt. Urgency statutes, statutes calling elections, and "statutes providing for tax levies or appropriations for usual current expenses of the State" (§9(a)) Verified1.
  • 90 days and 5% for a statewide referendum (§9(b)) Verified1.
  • Conflicts. If two approved measures conflict, "the provisions of the measure receiving the highest number of affirmative votes shall prevail" (§10(b)) Verified1.
  • The Legislature can't undo an initiative alone. It may amend an initiative statute only by a statute "that becomes effective only when approved by the electors unless the initiative statute permits amendment or repeal without the electors' approval" (§10(c)) Verified1.

Section 11: Local initiative and referendum

"Initiative and referendum powers may be exercised by the electors of each city or county under procedures that the Legislature shall provide" (§11(a)) Verified1. For a general law city like Williams, those procedures are in the Elections Code:

Power Constitution Statute for Williams
Initiative §8(a), §11(a) Petition signed by 10% of the city's registered voters; the council must adopt it, put it on the ballot, or order a report first (Elec. Code §9215) Verified5
Referendum §9(a), §11(a) Petition filed within 30 days of the date the adopted ordinance is attested by the city clerk, signed by 10% of the city's voters; the ordinance is suspended and the council must reconsider it (Elec. Code §9237) Verified6, and if it does not repeal it entirely, it must submit it to the voters (§9241) Verified7

Table source: Verified1

How courts read local power. "It is 'the duty of the courts to jealously guard this right of the people'" (DeVita v. County of Napa (1995) 9 Cal.4th 763) Verified8. The Court has said the power must "be broadly construed, resolving all doubts in favor of the reserved power" (Associated Home Builders etc., Inc. v. City of Livermore (1976) 18 Cal.3d 582) Verified9. When voters adopt a measure directly, "CEQA review is not required before direct adoption of an initiative" (Tuolumne Jobs & Small Business Alliance v. Superior Court (2014) 59 Cal.4th 1029) Verified10.

Taxes. Charter rules can limit referenda on taxes; Rossi v. Brown considered whether an initiative could repeal a tax under a charter that "prohibits referenda on tax ordinances" (Rossi v. Brown (1995) 9 Cal.4th 688) Verified11. Article XIII C, §3 separately protects initiatives "reducing or repealing any local tax, assessment, fee or charge" Verified1. See Article XIII C.

Section 12: No naming people or companies

Sections 13–19: Recall

  • No reason test. For state officers, the petition alleges a reason, but "Sufficiency of reason is not reviewable" (§14(a)) Verified1.
  • Local recall is a statute. "The Legislature shall provide for recall of local officers" (§19) Verified1. The Elections Code recall chapter governs "the recall of elective officers of ... all counties, cities" (§11000) Verified12.

Signatures to recall a Williams officer depend on the city's registered voters (Elec. Code §11221(a)) Verified13:

Registered voters in the city Signatures needed
Fewer than 1,000 30%
1,000 to 9,999 25%
10,000 to 49,999 20%
50,000 to 99,999 15%
100,000 or more 10%

Williams had 2,276 registered voters in the Secretary of State's September 4, 2026 report Verified14. That puts it in the 1,000 to 9,999 band, so a recall would need at least 569 signatures (25%) at that count Calculated14,15. The count that applies is the last report before the petition's form is approved, so confirm with the Colusa County elections office Verified15.

Section 20: When terms start

In Williams

  • Write a city law. Gather 10% of registered voters under Elec. Code §9215 Verified5. Keep it to one subject; Our reading: §8(d) applies to statewide measures, but courts and statutes apply similar ideas locally, so draft narrowly.
  • Block a new ordinance. You have 30 days from the date the clerk attests the adopted ordinance (§9237) Verified6. Urgency ordinances work differently; read the statute.
  • Recall. Follow Elec. Code §11000 and following Verified12.
  • Cut a fee or tax. XIII C §3 protects that initiative Verified1.

Common misconceptions

  • "City council elections are partisan." No. §6(a) makes city offices nonpartisan Verified1.
  • "Recall needs proof of wrongdoing." For state officers, "Sufficiency of reason is not reviewable" (§14(a)) Verified1.
  • "The Legislature can repeal any initiative." Not an initiative statute, without a vote, unless it allows that (§10(c)) Verified1.

Check yourself

Answer in your head first, then open each question.

Which city offices are nonpartisan?

All of them: "All judicial, school, county, and city offices ... shall be nonpartisan" (§6(a)) Verified1.

How many signatures does a statewide constitutional-amendment initiative need?

8% of the votes for all candidates for Governor at the last gubernatorial election (§8(b)) Verified1.

What is the test under the single-subject rule?

Whether all parts are "reasonably germane" to each other and the measure's purpose (Brosnahan) Verified2.

Where do Williams' initiative and referendum procedures come from?

The Elections Code, as §11(a) directs (e.g., §§9215, 9237) Verified1,5,6.
