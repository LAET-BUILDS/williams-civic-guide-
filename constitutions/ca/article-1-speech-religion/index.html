California Article I (part 2): Speech, petition, open government and religion

Lesson 2 of 68 · Speech, religion and public comment · 15 min read

On this page (7 sections)

  4. In Williams

These three sections are the ones a Williams resident is most likely to use: speaking at a council meeting, asking for records, petitioning the city, or objecting to religious favoritism.

Key takeaways

  • §2 protects speech as an affirmative right and reaches some private property, like large shopping centers.
  • §3(a) gives the right to "instruct" representatives, petition and assemble.
  • §3(b) makes open meetings and public records a constitutional right and tells courts to read access laws broadly.
  • §3(b)(7) requires every city to comply with the Brown Act and the Public Records Act.
  • §4 bans religious "preference" as well as establishment.

Section 2: Speech and press

A right, not just a limit. The First Amendment says what Congress may not do; §2(a) says "Every person may freely speak, write and publish" Verified1,2.

Shopping centers. In Robins v. Pruneyard Shopping Center (1979) 23 Cal.3d 899, the court held "sections 2 and 3 of article I of the California Constitution protect speech and petitioning, reasonably exercised, in shopping centers even when the centers are privately owned" Verified3. The U.S. Supreme Court let that stand (PruneYard Shopping Center v. Robins, 447 U.S. 74 (1980)) Verified4. Later, "the right to free speech granted by article I, section 2 of the California Constitution includes the right to urge customers in a shopping mall to boycott one of the stores in the mall" (Fashion Valley Mall, LLC v. NLRB (2007) 42 Cal.4th 850) Verified5.

Compelled speech. California's speech right can be "broader" than the First Amendment's. A marketing order that didn't implicate the First Amendment "does indeed implicate such a right under article I" (Gerawan Farming, Inc. v. Lyons (2000) 24 Cal.4th 468) Verified6.

Shield law (§2(b)). Journalists can't be held in contempt for refusing to disclose sources or unpublished information Verified1.

"Being responsible for the abuse." Our reading: the right doesn't protect defamation or other false statements of fact that harm someone.

Section 3: Petition, assembly and open government

§3(a): Instruct, petition, assemble. California adds the right "to instruct their representatives" Verified1. Public comment at council meetings is the most common way Williams residents do this; the Brown Act requires a chance to address the council on agenda items (Gov. Code §54954.3) Verified7.

§3(b)(1): Access is a constitutional right. "The meetings of public bodies and the writings of public officials and agencies shall be open to public scrutiny" Verified1. The section's current text shows "[As amended June 3, 2014.]" Verified1.

§3(b)(2): The tiebreaker. Access laws "shall be broadly construed if it furthers the people's right of access, and narrowly construed if it limits the right of access" Verified1. The Supreme Court applied it to hold that a county's GIS database is a public record: "such databases are public records that, unless otherwise exempt, must be" disclosed (Sierra Club v. Superior Court (2013) 57 Cal.4th 157) Verified8.

§3(b)(3)–(6): Limits. Access doesn't override privacy (§1), due process (§7), or existing exceptions such as law enforcement records Verified1.

§3(b)(7): Cities must comply. "Each local agency is hereby required to comply with the California Public Records Act ... and the Ralph M. Brown Act" Verified1. The cited CPRA location (Gov. Code §6250) has since been recodified; the act now starts at Gov. Code §7920.000 Verified9. Since the duty is in the constitution, a city can't refuse because the state hasn't paid for it. Our reading: that's the practical effect of putting the mandate in §3(b)(7) rather than leaving it to a reimbursable statute.

Private-device records. "Here, we hold that when a city employee uses a personal account to communicate about the conduct of public business, the writings may be subject to disclosure under the California Public Records Act" (City of San Jose v. Superior Court (2017) 2 Cal.5th 608) Verified10. Our reading: a Williams council member's texts about city business can be requested even if sent from a personal phone.

Section 4: Religion

"Without discrimination or preference." California bans preference, not just establishment. A key part of §4 "is identical to the establishment clause of the First Amendment" (Sands v. Morongo Unified School Dist. (1991) 53 Cal.3d 863) Verified11, but the preference clause adds more.

Government displays. The court ordered Los Angeles not to display "a lighted, single-barred cross on the Los Angeles City Hall" (Fox v. City of Los Angeles (1978) 22 Cal.3d 792) Verified12.

School graduation prayer. "Religious invocations and benedictions at public high school graduation ceremonies are constitutionally impermissible" (Sands v. Morongo Unified School Dist. (1991) 53 Cal.3d 863) Verified11.

Free exercise and neutral laws. A church-affiliated employer challenged the Women's Contraception Equity Act under both constitutions and lost (Catholic Charities of Sacramento, Inc. v. Superior Court (2004) 32 Cal.4th 527) Verified13.

Funding. Article XVI, §5 separately bans public money for any sectarian purpose; see Public finance Verified1.

In Williams

  • Public comment. §3(a) plus the Brown Act's comment right Verified7. The council can set reasonable time limits but can't bar criticism of itself (§54954.3(c)) Verified7.
  • Records. Ask the city clerk Verified14. The city must respond within 10 days (Gov. Code §7922.535) Verified15. Your reason for asking doesn't matter (§7921.300) Verified16. See the Public Records Act guide.
  • Invocations. Council prayer is governed federally by Town of Greece Verified17, but §4's "preference" language can reach further. Our reading: rotating, nonsectarian practices are safer under §4.
  • Sign rules. City sign ordinances must survive both §2 and the First Amendment. See First Amendment.

Common misconceptions

  • "Private property means no speech rights." Not for large shopping centers in California Verified3.
  • "Open government is just a statute the Legislature could repeal." It's in the constitution, and §3(b)(7) binds cities Verified1.
  • "Public comment means the council has to answer." The Brown Act generally bars action on non-agenda items; it doesn't require responses Verified18.

Check yourself

Pick an answer for each question. You will see right away if it is right, and why.

Question 1. What does §3(b)(2) tell courts to do with laws that limit access?

Show the answer and why

Construe them narrowly Verified1.

Question 2. Which two statutes does §3(b)(7) require every local agency to follow?

Show the answer and why

The California Public Records Act and the Ralph M. Brown Act Verified1.

Question 3. Can a privately owned shopping center in California ban all petitioning?

Show the answer and why

No. Robins v. Pruneyard protects reasonable speech and petitioning there Verified3.

Question 4. What does §4 ban that the First Amendment doesn't say expressly?

Show the answer and why

Religious "preference" Verified1.

Question 5. Spot the problem: What's missing from this sample agenda?

Show the answer and why

Answer: A chance for the public to address the council.

The Brown Act requires a chance to address the council on agenda items, and public comment is the most common way Williams residents use their right to petition. It doesn't require the council to respond or to vote on requests.
