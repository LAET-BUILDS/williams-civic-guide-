California Article III: The State, separation of powers, and who may refuse a statute

Lesson 60 of 68 · California's state government · 10 min read

On this page (9 sections)

Article III is short, but one section controls how every Williams official responds to a law they think is unconstitutional: they enforce it until a court says otherwise.

Key takeaways

  • California itself says the U.S. Constitution "is the supreme law of the land" (§1).
  • Three branches; a person in one "may not exercise either of the others" except as the constitution permits (§3).
  • Administrative agencies can't declare statutes unconstitutional or refuse to enforce them before an appellate court rules (§3.5).
  • Lockyer (2004): local executive officials "lacked authority" to stop enforcing a state statute on their own constitutional view.

Section 1: The U.S. Constitution is supreme

This repeats, in state law, what the federal Supremacy Clause already commands Verified2,1. See Article VI of the U.S. Constitution and How supremacy works.

Section 2: Boundaries and capital

Section 3: Separation of powers

Unlike the U.S. Constitution, California says this expressly Verified1. Courts read it flexibly. In Carmel Valley Fire Protection Dist. v. State (2001), the Court looked at the Legislature's role in state mandate claims and concluded "that no separation of powers violation has been demonstrated" (Carmel Valley Fire Protection Dist. v. State of California (2001) 25 Cal.4th 287) Verified3. In Professional Engineers v. Schwarzenegger (2010), the Governor's furlough authority depended on the Legislature: it existed "only if specifically grant[ed]" by statute or ratified, and the Court found the Legislature's 2009 budget revisions "operated to ratify" the furloughs (Professional Engineers in California Government v. Schwarzenegger (2010) 50 Cal.4th 989) Verified4.

Our reading: §3 governs state government. It doesn't by its own terms split a general law city's powers, which are set by statute (council, city manager, clerk) Verified1,5.

Section 3.5: Agencies can't nullify statutes

An "administrative agency ... has no power ... To declare a statute unconstitutional" (§3.5(b)) Verified1.

Lockyer v. City and County of San Francisco (2004)

The question was "whether a local executive official who is charged with the ministerial duty of enforcing a state statute exceeds his or her authority when, without any court having determined that the statute is unconstitutional, the official deliberately declines to enforce the statute" (Lockyer v. City and County of San Francisco (2004) 33 Cal.4th 1055) Verified6. The Court held "local executive officials lacked authority" to issue marriage licenses contrary to the statute then in force Verified6. Same-sex marriage was later recognized by the courts and is now in Article I, §7.5 Verified7,1.

Our reading: a Williams clerk or inspector with a ministerial duty under a state statute can't skip it on a personal view of the constitution. Officials can, and should, raise concerns with the city attorney; a court decides.

Sections 4–9: Salaries, suits, language, compensation

  • Salaries of elected state officers "may not be reduced during their term of office" (§4(a)) Verified1.
  • Suits against the State happen "in such manner and in such courts as shall be directed by law" (§5) Verified1. Our reading: that's why claims against public entities run through the Government Claims Act's procedures first.
  • English "is the official language of the State of California" (§6(b)) Verified1. §6 says it does not "supersede any of the rights guaranteed to the people by this Constitution" (§6(a)) Verified1.

In Williams

  • A city officer facing a state statute they think is unconstitutional still enforces it until a court rules (Lockyer) Verified6.
  • Residents can challenge the statute in court. That's the route §3.5 and Lockyer leave open Verified1,6.

Common misconceptions

  • "An official's oath lets them ignore laws they think are unconstitutional." Lockyer says no, for ministerial duties under state statute Verified6. See the oath.
  • "English-only means government can't use other languages." §6 doesn't say that; it states a policy and directs the Legislature to enforce it Verified1.

Check yourself

Pick an answer for each question. You will see right away if it is right, and why.

Question 1. What does Article III, §1 say about the U.S. Constitution?

Show the answer and why

That it "is the supreme law of the land" Verified1.

Question 2. Can a state agency refuse to enforce a statute because it thinks it is unconstitutional?

Show the answer and why

Not unless an appellate court has made that determination (§3.5(a)) Verified1.

Question 3. What did Lockyer v. San Francisco hold?

Show the answer and why

Local executive officials "lacked authority" to depart from the marriage statutes without a court ruling Verified6.

Question 4. Scenario: Suppose a Williams official thinks a state statute is unconstitutional. What should they do?

Show the answer and why

Answer: Keep enforcing it, raise concerns with the city attorney, and let a court decide.
