California Article I (part 3): Criminal justice rights

Lesson 20 of 68 · Police, searches and your day in court · 29 min read

California's criminal-justice rights look like the federal Fourth, Fifth, Sixth and Eighth Amendments, but voters have changed them several times. The biggest change: since 1982's "Truth-in-Evidence" rule, California courts generally can't exclude evidence unless federal law requires it.

Key takeaways

  • §13 matches the Fourth Amendment almost word for word, and covers code-enforcement entries.
  • §17 bans "cruel or unusual" punishment and excessive fines; it applies to city penalties.
  • §28(f)(2) means evidence is excluded only when federal law requires it (In re Lance W.).
  • §24's sentence forcing state courts to follow federal rules for all criminal rights was struck down as an invalid revision (Raven), but its "consistent with" sentence remains in the text.
  • City code violations prosecuted as misdemeanors or infractions get these protections.

Section 12: Bail

Bail is a right except for listed serious crimes. Courts may also release a person "on his or her own recognizance" at their discretion Verified1. See the Eighth Amendment.

Section 13: Searches and seizures

Before 1982, California courts read §13 more broadly than the Fourth Amendment. In People v. Brisendine (1975), officers could pat down for weapons, but "the officers' subsequent intrusion into the opaque bottle and envelopes inside the knapsack cannot be justified by the limited purpose which validated the search in its inception" (People v. Brisendine (1975) 13 Cal.3d 528) Verified2.

After 1982, §28(f)(2) limited exclusion to what federal law requires; see §28.

Code enforcement. "In the absence of consent or exigent circumstances, government officials engaged in the abatement of a public nuisance must have a warrant to enter any private property where such entry would invade a constitutionally protected privacy interest" (Gleaves v. Waters (1985) 175 Cal.App.3d 413) Verified3. Inspection warrants require cause under Code Civ. Proc. §1822.52 Verified4. See Williams: entry and the Fourth Amendment.

Section 14: Charging felonies

Felonies are charged by grand jury indictment or by a prosecutor's "information" after a preliminary hearing. That's allowed because the federal grand jury right isn't incorporated (Hurtado v. California, 110 U.S. 516 (1884)) Verified5.

Section 15: Defendants' rights

Speedy public trial, witnesses, counsel, confrontation, no double jeopardy, no self-incrimination, due process Verified1. State due process protects against "arbitrary adjudicative procedures" (People v. Ramirez (1979) 25 Cal.3d 260) Verified6.

Section 16: Jury trial

  • Civil juries. Unlike the Seventh Amendment, which doesn't bind states, §16 guarantees civil jury trial in California, with three-fourths agreement enough for a civil verdict Verified1.
  • Representative juries. "The use of peremptory challenges to remove prospective jurors on the sole ground of group bias violates the right to trial by a jury drawn from a representative cross-section of the community under article I, section 16" (People v. Wheeler (1978) 22 Cal.3d 258) Verified7.
  • Misdemeanor juries. §16 sets jury size for misdemeanors; see the text Verified1.

Section 17: Cruel or unusual punishment; excessive fines

"Or," not "and." The federal Eighth Amendment says "cruel and unusual" Verified8.

Fines. "The touchstone of the constitutional inquiry under the Excessive Fines Clause is the principle of proportionality," weighing "(1) the defendant's culpability; (2) the relationship between the harm and the penalty; (3) the penalties imposed in similar statutes; and (4) the defendant's ability to pay" (People ex rel. Lockyer v. R.J. Reynolds Tobacco Co. (2005) 37 Cal.4th 707) Verified9.

Daily penalties. In Hale v. Morgan (1978) 22 Cal.3d 388, a $100-a-day penalty had reached $17,300. Such a "mandatory, fixed, substantial and cumulative punitive sanction against persons of such disparate culpability is manifestly suspect" Verified10. See Williams: fines.

Ability to pay. People v. Dueñas (2019), a Court of Appeal decision, held that "due process of law requires the trial court to conduct an ability to pay hearing" before imposing certain court assessments (People v. Dueñas (2019) 30 Cal.App.5th 1157) Verified11. Other appellate courts have disagreed about its reach; we haven't surveyed them, so read the later cases before relying on it Unverified12.

Section 18: Treason

Section 23: Grand juries

Every county, including Colusa, must summon at least one grand jury a year Verified1. Our reading: California civil grand juries also investigate local government; check the Penal Code for their watchdog role over cities.

Section 24: Independent rights and the criminal-case limit

First sentence: state rights stand on their own Verified1.

Second paragraph: added by Proposition 115 (1990). The printed text includes the sentence that "This Constitution shall not be construed by the courts to afford greater rights to criminal defendants than those afforded by the Constitution of the United States" Verified1. The California Supreme Court held "that new article I, section 24, represents an invalid revision of the California Constitution" but "its invalidity does not affect the remaining provisions of Proposition 115" (Raven v. Deukmejian (1990) 52 Cal.3d 336) Verified13. Our reading: the paragraph still appears in the official publication but was held invalid; don't rely on it.

Section 27: Death penalty

Section 28: Victims' Bill of Rights and Truth-in-Evidence

The section's centerpiece is a list of victims' rights in §28(b). For everyone else, the key part is §28(f)(2), Truth-in-Evidence: "relevant evidence shall not be excluded in any criminal proceeding," with listed exceptions Verified1. The court held this "abrogated both the 'vicarious exclusionary rule' ... and a defendant's right" to exclude evidence under state-only search rules (In re Lance W. (1985) 37 Cal.3d 873) Verified14. Our reading: Brisendine-style broader state rules may still define what's unlawful, but evidence generally comes in unless federal law requires exclusion Verified14.

Sections 29, 30 and 32

§29 gives the people due process and a speedy trial. §30 allows joinder and some hearsay at preliminary hearings. §32, adopted by initiative November 8, 2016, provides parole consideration for nonviolent felony offenders Verified1.

In Williams

  • Code prosecutions. A Williams ordinance violation can be charged as a misdemeanor or infraction (Gov. Code §36900) Verified15. The defendant gets §§15–17 protections. See Williams: charging.
  • Inspections. Entry without consent or emergency needs a warrant Verified3,4.
  • Fines. Daily accruing penalties face §17 and due process limits Verified10,9.

Common misconceptions

  • "California's search rules exclude more evidence than federal rules." Not since 1982 Verified14.
  • "§24 bars state courts from giving defendants more rights." That sentence was held invalid in Raven Verified13.

Check yourself

Answer in your head first, then open each question.

What changed about suppressing evidence in California in 1982?

Truth-in-Evidence (§28(f)(2)) limited exclusion to what federal law requires (In re Lance W.) Verified14.

Does §17 apply to city civil penalties?

Yes, it bars excessive fines; courts weigh culpability, harm, similar penalties and ability to pay Verified9.

Why can a California prosecutor charge a felony without a grand jury?

§14 allows charging by information, and the federal grand jury right isn't incorporated Verified5.

Does a code inspector need a warrant to enter private property for nuisance abatement?

Yes, absent consent or exigent circumstances (Gleaves v. Waters) Verified3.
