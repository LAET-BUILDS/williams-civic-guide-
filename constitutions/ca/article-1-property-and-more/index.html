California Article I (part 4): Property, fishing, and the full section map

California's takings clause covers more than the Fifth Amendment: property "taken or damaged." That word matters every time a city project floods, cracks or undermines private property.

Key takeaways

  • §19(a): property may be "taken or damaged" for public use only with just compensation, paid first.
  • Physical damage caused by a public improvement as designed is compensable, foreseeable or not (Albers).
  • §19(b) bars using eminent domain on an owner-occupied home to convey it to a private person, with exceptions.
  • Generally applicable development conditions aren't "exactions" under state or federal takings law (CBIA v. San Jose).

Section 19: Eminent domain

"Or damaged." The Fifth Amendment covers property "taken" Verified2. California adds "damaged" Verified1.

Inverse condemnation. When government damages property without starting eminent domain, owners can sue. "Any actual physical injury to real property proximately caused by the improvement as deliberately designed" is compensable "whether foreseeable or not," subject to exceptions (Albers v. County of Los Angeles (1965) 62 Cal.2d 250) Verified3. Public entities "may be liable on an inverse condemnation theory for the alleged physical damage to plaintiffs' property proximately caused by the excavation as deliberately planned and designed" (Holtz v. Superior Court (1970) 3 Cal.3d 296) Verified4. (Those cases cite the older numbering, §14 Verified4.)

Development conditions. The U.S. Supreme Court's Nollan/Dolan tests apply to exactions Verified5,6. In San Remo Hotel (2002), the Court of Appeal had applied that review to housing replacement fees "because they were exacted discretionarily and applied only to a relatively small number of property owners" (San Remo Hotel v. City and County of San Francisco (2002) 27 Cal.4th 643) Verified7; the Supreme Court reversed on that point Verified7. In CBIA v. City of San Jose (2015), inclusionary housing conditions "do not impose 'exactions' upon the developers' property so as to bring into play the unconstitutional conditions doctrine under the takings clause of the federal or state Constitution" (California Building Industry Assn. v. City of San Jose (2015) 61 Cal.4th 435) Verified8. The U.S. Supreme Court later held legislatively imposed fees aren't exempt from Nollan/Dolan (Sheetz v. County of El Dorado, 601 U.S. 267 (2024)) Verified9.

Deadlines. A takings claim tied to a subdivision ordinance is governed by the short limitations period of Gov. Code §66499.37 (Hensler v. City of Glendale (1994) 8 Cal.4th 1) Verified10.

§19(b)–(e): Homes. Added June 3, 2008, by initiative Verified1. Federal law allowed such transfers for economic development in Kelo (Kelo v. City of New London, 545 U.S. 469 (2005)) Verified11. "Local government" includes "any city" (§19(e)(2)) Verified1. Exceptions: public health and safety, serious repeated crime, emergencies, contamination, and public works Verified1.

In Williams

  • Public works damage. If a city sewer, storm drain or street project as designed physically damages your property, §19 inverse condemnation is the claim Verified3. Government claims deadlines apply; Our reading: file the claim promptly and get legal advice.
  • Development fees and conditions. Williams impact fees must also follow the Mitigation Fee Act Verified12 and federal exactions limits Verified9. See Williams: fees.
  • Homes. Williams can't condemn an owner-occupied home to hand it to a private developer, outside the exceptions Verified1. See Williams: property.

Common misconceptions

  • "Only a total taking counts." California also compensates physical damage Verified3.
  • "After Kelo, cities can take any home for private development." California's §19(b) bars that for owner-occupied homes, with exceptions Verified1.

Question 1. What word does California's takings clause add to the Fifth Amendment's?

Show the answer and why

"Damaged" Verified1.

Question 2. Is damage from a public improvement compensable if it wasn't foreseeable?

Show the answer and why

Yes, under Albers, if caused by the improvement as deliberately designed, subject to exceptions Verified3.

Question 3. What does §19(b) prohibit?

Show the answer and why

Using eminent domain on an owner-occupied residence to convey it to a private person Verified1.

Question 4. Scenario: Suppose a city storm drain project, built as designed, cracks the foundation of a nearby home. What claim fits?

Show the answer and why

Answer: Inverse condemnation under §19, because property was "damaged" for public use.
