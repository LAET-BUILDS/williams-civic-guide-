Final quiz

These 25 questions come from across the Constitution Course. Pick an answer for each question. You will see right away if it is right. Open "Show the answer and why" to see the lesson sentence it comes from. Your answers are not saved or sent.

Question 1. Scenario: During public comment, a resident criticizes how a council member voted on water rates. The mayor cuts her off, citing a council rule against "personal attacks." Which is right?

Show the answer and why

Answer: Not allowed. A council can stop disruption, but not criticism of its members' votes.

A council can't ban "personal attacks" during public comment. The Brown Act bars prohibiting criticism of the body's acts, and a member's vote is one of them. Disruption, not criticism, is what a council may stop. A rule that treats everyone alike is still invalid if what it bans is criticism.

From this lesson: “Disruption, not criticism, is what a council may stop.” Lesson 1: First Amendment: Religion, speech, press, assembly and petition

Question 2. Scenario: You ask the city for a council member's texts about a city contract. The clerk says the texts are on his personal phone, so they aren't public records. Which is right?

Show the answer and why

Answer: They may still be public records. What counts is that they're about city business.

In City of San Jose (2017), the California Supreme Court held that when a city employee uses a personal account to communicate about public business, the writings may be subject to disclosure under the Public Records Act. And you never have to say why you want a record.

From this lesson: “when a city employee uses a personal account to communicate about the conduct of public business, the writings may be subject to disclosure under the California Public Records Act” Lesson 2: California Article I (part 2): Speech, petition, open government and religion

Question 3. Scenario: You ask the City of Williams for its latest water rate study. Ten days later, what must the city have done, at minimum?

Show the answer and why

Answer: A decision on whether it has disclosable records, or a written extension notice

The Public Records Act requires a determination within 10 days: does the request seek disclosable records? The agency can extend that "in unusual circumstances" by written notice. Ten days is the deadline to decide, not always to hand everything over. And there are no limits on access based on why you want a record.

From this lesson: “The CPRA: a right to inspect, copies for direct cost, a 10-day determination, and no questions about why you want it.” Lesson 12: From constitution to statute: the laws that carry your rights into City Hall

Question 4. Put the steps in order: Residents want to stop a new Williams ordinance by referendum. Put the steps in order.

Use the Up and Down buttons to change the order, then press Check.

  1. The ordinance is suspended and the council must reconsider it
  2. The council adopts the ordinance
  3. If the council doesn't repeal it entirely, it goes to the voters
  4. Within 30 days, residents file a petition signed by 10% of the city's voters
  5. The city clerk attests the adopted ordinance

Show the answer and why

The right order:

  1. The council adopts the ordinance
  2. The city clerk attests the adopted ordinance
  3. Within 30 days, residents file a petition signed by 10% of the city's voters
  4. The ordinance is suspended and the council must reconsider it
  5. If the council doesn't repeal it entirely, it goes to the voters

The 30 days run from the date the clerk attests the adopted ordinance, not from the first reading. A valid petition, signed by 10% of the city's voters, suspends the ordinance. The council must reconsider it, and if it does not repeal it entirely, it must submit it to the voters (Elec. Code §§9237, 9241).

From this lesson: “the ordinance is suspended and the council must reconsider it” Lesson 8: California Article II: Voting, initiative, referendum and recall

Question 5. Williams had 2,276 registered voters in the September 2026 state report. At that count, how many signatures would a petition to recall a council member need?

Show the answer and why

Answer: 25%, at least 569 signatures

Recall signatures depend on the city's registered voters (Elec. Code §11221). A city with 1,000 to 9,999 voters needs 25%, so at least 569 for Williams at that count. The 10% figure is for city initiatives and referendums; 30% is for cities under 1,000 voters. Confirm the count that applies with the Colusa County elections office.

From this lesson: “That puts it in the 1,000 to 9,999 band, so a recall would need at least 569 signatures (25%) at that count” Lesson 8: California Article II: Voting, initiative, referendum and recall

Question 6. Match: Match each goal with the tool that fits it.

Show the answer and why
  • Stop an ordinance the council adopted last week before it takes effect: Referendum petition
  • Put a new city law on the ballot that the council never proposed: Initiative petition
  • Cut the sewer charge, even though it was adopted after a proper Prop 218 hearing: Initiative petition
  • Remove a council member before the term ends: Recall petition
  • Undo a council vote taken at a meeting that broke the Brown Act: Written cure-and-correct demand

A referendum can hold a new ordinance before it takes effect. An initiative can write a new law, and Article XIII C, §3 lets voters reduce or repeal a local fee or charge by initiative. Recall of city officers comes from Art. II, §19. Before suing over a Brown Act violation, send a cure-and-correct demand.

From this lesson: “if the council passes an ordinance residents oppose, a referendum petition can hold it before it takes effect” Lesson 14: How the constitutions reach the Williams code

Question 7. Scenario: A Williams resident is two months behind on her city water bill. A city tax measure is on the ballot. Can she vote on it, and what protects that?

Show the answer and why

Answer: Yes, under Harper and the Fourteenth Amendment.

The Twenty-fourth Amendment's text covers only federal elections. For state and city elections, Harper (1966) bars any wealth or fee requirement under the Fourteenth Amendment. So a resident behind on a utility bill can still vote on council races and local tax measures.

From this lesson: “For state and city elections, *Harper* (1966) bars any wealth or fee requirement under the Fourteenth Amendment” Lesson 5: Twenty-fourth Amendment: No poll taxes in federal elections

Question 8. Spot the problem: What is wrong with that reasoning?

Show the answer and why

Answer: Williams has no charter, so it has no "municipal affairs" shield

Only charter cities get home rule, and only for municipal affairs. Williams has been a general law city since 1920. It has no charter, so the "municipal affairs" protection of §5 doesn't apply, and a rule that contradicts state law is preempted. But preemption "is not lightly presumed": a state law on a subject doesn't by itself bar the city from acting.

From this lesson: “It has no charter, so the "municipal affairs" protection of §5 doesn't apply.” Lesson 9: California Article XI: Local government, and why Williams can regulate at all

Question 9. Spot the problem: What is wrong with the staff report?

Show the answer and why

Answer: Money for specific purposes makes it a special tax, needing two-thirds.

Every local tax is general or special. A "special tax" means any tax imposed for specific purposes, including one placed into a general fund. A general tax needs a majority of voters; a special tax needs two-thirds (Art. XIII C, §2(b), (d)).

From this lesson: “means any tax imposed for specific purposes, including a tax imposed for specific purposes, which is placed into a general fund” Lesson 22: California Article XIII C: Voter approval for local taxes (Proposition 218)

Question 10. Scenario: The council wants to put a new general tax on a special election ballot in March, instead of waiting for a regular council election. When can it do that?

Show the answer and why

Answer: Only if the council declares an emergency by a unanimous vote

A general tax needs a majority vote, and the election must be held with a regular council election unless the council declares an emergency by a unanimous vote (§2(b)). The vote needed stays a majority either way. (Upland held this timing rule binds local governments, not citizen initiatives.)

From this lesson: “General tax: majority vote, at a regular council election unless there's a unanimous emergency declaration (§2(b)).” Lesson 22: California Article XIII C: Voter approval for local taxes (Proposition 218)

Question 11. Scenario: By resolution, the council raises the amounts of its administrative citation fines. A resident says that's a new tax that needs a vote of the people. Which is right?

Show the answer and why

Answer: No vote is needed, but the fines are still limited by the excessive fines clauses.

"Tax" means almost any charge a local government imposes, minus seven exceptions. A fine or penalty "imposed ... as a result of a violation of law" is one (§1(e)(5)). So fines set by resolution don't trigger the vote rules, but they're still limited by the federal and state Excessive Fines Clauses.

From this lesson: “Fines and penalties for violating the law are one exception (§1(e)(5)).” Lesson 22: California Article XIII C: Voter approval for local taxes (Proposition 218)

Question 12. The city wants to charge only the parcels on one street for new sidewalks that specially benefit them, as a Prop 218 assessment. What does that take?

Show the answer and why

Answer: An engineer's report and mailed ballots weighted by cost; it fails if "no" ballots outweigh "yes"

Assessments need a special benefit, an engineer's report, and a mailed ballot weighted by cost. A majority protest exists if the "no" ballots exceed the "yes" ballots (§4(e)). The notice-and-written-protest steps are the separate rule for property-related fees (§6(a)). And "General enhancement of property value does not constitute 'special benefit'."

From this lesson: “Assessments need a special benefit, an engineer's report, and a mailed ballot weighted by cost; a majority protest kills them (§4).” Lesson 23: California Article XIII D: Assessments and property-related fees (Proposition 218)

Question 13. Scenario: The city mails notice of a water rate increase and holds the hearing 45 days later. Written protests come from 40% of the parcels. Many others speak against it at the hearing but file nothing. Some ask for an election. What happens?

Show the answer and why

Answer: The council can adopt it. Water rates need no election, and 40% isn't a majority.

A new or higher property-related fee fails if written protests come from a majority of owners of the identified parcels (§6(a)). Spoken objections don't count toward that. Water, sewer and refuse fees skip the separate election that other property-related fees need (§6(c)). The fee still can't bring in more than the service costs (§6(b)).

From this lesson: “Water, sewer and refuse fees skip the separate election; others need one (§6(c)).” Lesson 23: California Article XIII D: Assessments and property-related fees (Proposition 218)

Question 14. Match: Who has the power over each of these?

Show the answer and why
  • PG&E's electric rates in Williams: The state Public Utilities Commission
  • Rates for the city-owned water system: The City of Williams
  • Rates charged by a privately owned water company: The state Public Utilities Commission
  • Granting a utility a city franchise: The City of Williams

Private utilities, including private water companies, are "public utilities subject to control by the Legislature," and the PUC sets their rates. A city may not regulate what the Legislature gives the PUC, but it keeps its franchise power (§8). A city-owned water system rests on Art. XI, §9, and its rates go through the city under Article XIII D.

From this lesson: “A complaint about PG&E or a phone carrier's rates goes to the PUC, not the City Council (§§6, 8)” Lesson 28: California Article XII: Public utilities

Question 15. Scenario: The city plans to shut off a family's water for an unpaid bill the family says is wrong. Under due process, what must come first?

Show the answer and why

Answer: A way to dispute the bill and a hearing before someone who can fix errors

Under Memphis Light v. Craft, before shutting off service for nonpayment, the city must give notice that explains how to dispute the bill and a chance to be heard by someone with authority to fix errors. Due process applies to any government deprivation, not only in court. But it calls for a fair process, not necessarily a trial.

From this lesson: “before shutting off service for nonpayment, the city must give notice that explains how to dispute the bill and a chance to be heard by someone with authority to fix errors” Lesson 29: Fourteenth Amendment: Citizenship, due process and equal protection

Question 16. Scenario: A city code officer visits a Williams home. There's no warrant, no consent and no emergency. Which of these does the officer need consent or a warrant for?

Show the answer and why

Answer: Walking around to the back yard to look for violations

What's in plain view from a public place isn't a search. Walking up to the door is allowed under the implicit license; walking around the back yard isn't. The yard right around a house (curtilage) is protected, and code inspectors need consent or a warrant to enter protected areas (Camara).

From this lesson: “Allowed under the implicit license; walking around the back yard isn't” Lesson 15: Fourth Amendment: Searches, seizures and warrants

Question 17. Scenario: A Williams owner gets a $100-a-day administrative fine for a code violation. It runs for six months and reaches about $18,000, for a problem that caused little harm. What is her strongest constitutional argument?

Show the answer and why

Answer: The total is grossly disproportional to the harm, even if each day's fine is fair.

The Excessive Fines Clause binds cities (Timbs), and it covers civil penalties that punish. A fine is excessive if it is "grossly disproportional to the gravity of the offense." A fine that's reasonable per day can become excessive in total, so courts look at the total imposed on this person for this harm, not at a dollar line.

From this lesson: “A fine that's reasonable per day can become excessive in total.” Lesson 19: Eighth Amendment: Bail, fines and punishment

Question 18. Scenario: A new city storm drain, working just as designed, sends water that cracks a resident's foundation. No one saw it coming. Which is right?

Show the answer and why

Answer: Inverse condemnation under §19; she should file a claim with the city promptly.

California's §19 covers property "taken or damaged" for public use. Physical damage caused by a public improvement as designed is compensable, foreseeable or not (Albers). If a city sewer, storm drain or street project damages your property, inverse condemnation is the claim, and government claim deadlines apply.

From this lesson: “Physical damage caused by a public improvement as designed is compensable, foreseeable or not” Lesson 33: California Article I (part 4): Property, fishing, and the full section map

Question 19. Scenario: A city truck backs into your parked car. You want the City of Williams to pay for the repair. Under state law, what comes first?

Show the answer and why

Answer: A written claim to the city within six months

The Eleventh Amendment protects the State of California, not the City of Williams. But before suing a public entity for money on most state-law claims, you first present a written claim. For injury to a person or to personal property, like a car, it's due "not later than six months after the accrual of the cause of action"; most other claims get one year (Gov. Code §911.2).

From this lesson: “not later than six months after the accrual of the cause of action” Lesson 57: Eleventh Amendment: Suits against states

Question 20. Scenario: A Williams city employee is sure that a state statute she must apply is unconstitutional. No court has ruled on it. What should happen?

Show the answer and why

Answer: She keeps applying it. A resident who agrees can challenge it in court.

In Lockyer (2004), local executive officials "lacked authority" to stop enforcing a state statute on their own constitutional view. A city officer still enforces it until a court rules. Residents can challenge the statute in court; that's the route Lockyer leaves open.

From this lesson: “A city officer facing a state statute they think is unconstitutional still enforces it until a court rules” Lesson 60: California Article III: The State, separation of powers, and who may refuse a statute

Question 21. Scenario: In January, a resident commits a code violation that is charged as an infraction. In March, the council raises the fine for that infraction. The case is heard in April. Which fine applies?

Show the answer and why

Answer: The old one, since a criminal penalty can't be raised after the fact

Neither Congress nor a state can pass an ex post facto law, and a city acts with state power, so the limit reaches it. If the council raises the penalty for a code violation, the higher penalty can't be applied to a violation that happened before the change, to the extent the penalty is criminal (misdemeanors and infractions). Civil fees and administrative charges are analyzed differently.

From this lesson: “the higher penalty can't be applied to a violation that happened before the change” Lesson 46: Article I, Sections 9–10: What Congress and the states may not do

Question 22. Which statement about Williams and federal power is correct?

Show the answer and why

Answer: Grant conditions are allowed; ordering city staff to run a federal program isn't.

Valid federal law beats city law, but under Printz Washington can't order Williams staff to enforce a federal program; it can offer money with conditions instead. State law beats city law too, but that comes from California's own constitution (Art. XI, §7), not the Supremacy Clause.

From this lesson: “Congress can't order states or cities to carry out federal programs (the anti-commandeering rule).” Lesson 48: Article VI: The supreme law of the land

Question 23. A statewide group wants to change California's constitution in a way that would rewrite the basic plan of state government (a "revision"). Which route is NOT open to them?

Show the answer and why

Answer: Gathering signatures to put it on the ballot as an initiative

The Legislature can propose an amendment or a revision by two-thirds of each house (§1), and voters can call a convention to revise (§2). "The electors may amend the Constitution by initiative" (§3), but an initiative can amend, not revise. Every change still needs a majority of voters (§4).

From this lesson: “An initiative can amend, not revise.” Lesson 67: California Article XVIII: Amending and revising the Constitution

Question 24. Scenario: A new bar wants to open in downtown Williams. Which is correct?

Show the answer and why

Answer: The State licenses its alcohol sales; the city mainly decides where it can operate.

California's constitution gives the State "the exclusive right and power to license and regulate" alcohol (Art. XX, §22). A bar in Williams is licensed by the State, not the city. The city's role is land use: cities generally regulate where businesses can operate through zoning and permits. Repeal handed the question back to the states, not to cities.

From this lesson: “Cities generally regulate where businesses can operate through zoning and permits” Lesson 38: Twenty-first Amendment: Repeal of Prohibition

Question 25. Scenario: The county sells a Williams home over about $15,000 in unpaid property taxes. It sells for $40,000. Who gets the extra $25,000?

Show the answer and why

Answer: The former owner who lost the home

In Tyler v. Hennepin County (2023), a county sold a condo over a tax debt of about $15,000 and kept the $25,000 surplus. The Supreme Court said "A taxpayer is entitled to the surplus in excess of the debt owed." Government can't keep more than it's owed in a tax sale. Proper notice doesn't change that.

From this lesson: “"A taxpayer is entitled to the surplus in excess of the debt owed."” Lesson 68: The cases behind the rights, and where to read the originals
