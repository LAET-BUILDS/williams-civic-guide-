Final quiz

These 18 questions come from across the Constitution Course. Pick an answer for each question. You will see right away if it is right. Open "Show the answer and why" to see the lesson sentence it comes from. Nothing is saved or sent.

Question 1. Who does the First Amendment's free speech rule bind?

Show the answer and why

Answer: Only the government

It limits government. A private store or employer is not bound by it.

From this lesson: “Who it binds: government only. A private store or employer isn't bound by it” Lesson 1: First Amendment: Religion, speech, press, assembly and petition

Question 2. In California, what makes open meetings and public records a right?

Show the answer and why

Answer: The California Constitution (Art. I, §3(b))

Article I, §3(b) of the state constitution makes them a constitutional right. Courts must read access laws broadly.

From this lesson: “§3(b) makes open meetings and public records a constitutional right and tells courts to read access laws broadly.” Lesson 2: California Article I (part 2): Speech, petition, open government and religion

Question 3. Do Williams City Council candidates run as Democrats or Republicans?

Show the answer and why

Answer: No. City offices are nonpartisan.

Every city office in California, including Williams council seats, is nonpartisan.

From this lesson: “Every city office, including Williams council seats, is nonpartisan (§6).” Lesson 8: California Article II: Voting, initiative, referendum and recall

Question 4. Williams is a general law city. What does that mean?

Show the answer and why

Answer: It has no charter of its own

A general law city has no charter. So it follows the state's general laws closely.

From this lesson: “It has no charter, so the "municipal affairs" protection of §5 doesn't apply.” Lesson 9: California Article XI: Local government, and why Williams can regulate at all

Question 5. Spot the problem: What is the problem with this ordinance?

Show the answer and why

Answer: It duplicates state law, so it is preempted

A city law is preempted if it duplicates state law, contradicts it, or enters a field the state fully covers.

From this lesson: “A local law is preempted if it duplicates, contradicts, or enters a field fully occupied by state law.” Lesson 10: State law over city law: what a general law city can and can't do

Question 6. Scenario: A city inspector asks to look inside your home. You say no, and there is no emergency. What does the inspector need to come in?

Show the answer and why

Answer: A warrant

City health and safety inspectors need your consent or a warrant to enter a home.

From this lesson: “Since 1967, city health and safety inspectors need consent or a warrant to enter homes and non-public parts of businesses” Lesson 15: Fourth Amendment: Searches, seizures and warrants

Question 7. When can the city take private land, for example for a road?

Show the answer and why

Answer: Only for public use, and it must pay just compensation

The Takings Clause binds cities. Land can be taken only for public use, and the owner must be paid fairly.

From this lesson: “Property can be taken only for public use and with just compensation.” Lesson 16: Fifth Amendment: Grand juries, double jeopardy, self-incrimination, due process and takings

Question 8. When is a fine too big under the Eighth Amendment?

Show the answer and why

Answer: When it is grossly out of proportion to how serious the offense is

A fine is excessive if it is "grossly disproportional to the gravity of the offense."

From this lesson: “A fine is excessive if "grossly disproportional to the gravity of the offense"” Lesson 19: Eighth Amendment: Bail, fines and punishment

Question 9. Prop 13 caps the basic property tax at what?

Show the answer and why

Answer: 1% of the property's full cash value, plus voter-approved debt

Property tax can't be more than 1% of full cash value, plus debt that voters approved.

From this lesson: “Property tax can't exceed "One percent (1%) of the full cash value" (§1(a)), plus voter-approved debt (§1(b)).” Lesson 21: California Article XIII A: Proposition 13, the 1% cap and two-thirds votes

Question 10. What does a new city general tax need?

Show the answer and why

Answer: A majority vote of the voters

A general tax needs a majority of voters, usually at a regular council election.

From this lesson: “General tax: majority vote, at a regular council election unless there's a unanimous emergency declaration (§2(b)).” Lesson 22: California Article XIII C: Voter approval for local taxes (Proposition 218)

Question 11. Put the steps in order: The city wants to raise the sewer fee. Put the steps in order.

Use the Up and Down buttons to change the order, then press Check.

  1. At least 45 days later, the city holds a public hearing
  2. The city mails a notice to property owners
  3. The city counts the written protests
  4. If there is no majority protest, the council can adopt the fee

Show the answer and why

The right order:

  1. The city mails a notice to property owners
  2. At least 45 days later, the city holds a public hearing
  3. The city counts the written protests
  4. If there is no majority protest, the council can adopt the fee

New or higher property fees need mailed notice, a hearing at least 45 days later, and no majority written protest.

From this lesson: “New or higher property-related fees need mailed notice, a hearing at least 45 days later, and no majority written protest (§6(a)).” Lesson 23: California Article XIII D: Assessments and property-related fees (Proposition 218)

Question 12. Does the Fourteenth Amendment limit the City of Williams?

Show the answer and why

Answer: Yes. It limits states and everything they create, including cities.

"No State shall..." covers states and what they create: cities, counties and school districts.

From this lesson: “"No State shall ..." It limits states and everything they create, including cities, counties and school districts.” Lesson 29: Fourteenth Amendment: Citizenship, due process and equal protection

Question 13. Scenario: A 16-year-old U.S. citizen in Williams wants to get ready to vote. What can they do now?

Show the answer and why

Answer: Preregister to vote

A 16- or 17-year-old can preregister. Anyone who will be 18 by the next election can register for it.

From this lesson: “In Williams: a 16- or 17-year-old can preregister, and anyone who will be 18 by the next election can register for it” Lesson 6: Twenty-sixth Amendment: Voting at 18

Question 14. A city rule conflicts with a valid federal law. Which one wins?

Show the answer and why

Answer: The federal law

The U.S. Constitution and valid federal laws are "the supreme Law of the Land." State and city law that conflicts loses.

From this lesson: “State and city law that conflicts loses.” Lesson 49: Supremacy: which law wins when federal, state and city law collide

Question 15. To sue in federal court, you need "standing." What is it?

Show the answer and why

Answer: A real injury caused by the other side that a court can fix

Federal courts hear only real cases. You need a real injury, caused by the defendant, that a court can fix.

From this lesson: “To sue, you need standing: a real injury caused by the defendant that a court can fix.” Lesson 56: Article III: The federal courts

Question 16. Scenario: After a hearing, the city makes a decision you think breaks the law. What is the usual way to ask a court to step in?

Show the answer and why

Answer: File a petition for a writ of mandate in superior court

Challenging a city decision usually means a writ of mandate in superior court. Deadlines are short, so get advice.

From this lesson: “Challenging a city decision usually means a petition for writ of mandate in superior court (§10)” Lesson 63: California Article VI: The Judiciary

Question 17. Match: Match each change with what it needs.

Show the answer and why
  • Approve any change to the California Constitution: A majority of voters
  • Ratify an amendment to the U.S. Constitution: Three-fourths of the states
  • The Legislature proposes a California amendment: Two-thirds of each house

Every change to the California Constitution needs a majority of voters. The Legislature proposes one by two-thirds of each house. The U.S. Constitution needs three-fourths of the states.

From this lesson: “Every change needs a majority of voters (§4). The U.S. Constitution needs three-fourths of the states.” Lesson 67: California Article XVIII: Amending and revising the Constitution

Question 18. How long must a state bill be in print before the final vote?

Show the answer and why

Answer: 72 hours

Bills must be in print 72 hours before a final vote, so the public can read them.

From this lesson: “Bills must be in print 72 hours before a final vote (§8(b)(2))” Lesson 61: California Article IV: The Legislature
