A worked path: from a Williams code section up to both constitutions

Lesson 13 of 68 · Local government and the city council · 6 min read

This lesson traces two real Williams Municipal Code (WMC) sections up the ladder of law, step by step, so you can do the same with any section.

Key takeaways

  • Start with the exact code words, then go up one rung at a time: state statute, California Constitution, U.S. Constitution.
  • At each rung, ask: what does this rule require, and does the code section meet it on its face or only as applied?
  • End with what would settle it and which records would show the facts.

The method

  1. Quote the section exactly. Use the official code (Municode) or the city clerk Verified1,2.
  2. Find the state statute on the same subject. Is the section preempted or required by it? (State law and the city)
  3. Find the California clause. Article I for rights; XI for power; XIII C/D for money.
  4. Find the federal clause and check incorporation (Incorporation).
  5. Find the leading cases for each clause.
  6. Write "Our reading": facial question vs. as-applied question.
  7. List the records that would answer the as-applied question.

Example 1: water-chapter entry

Code. The director has "the right of entering any or all premises for the purpose of determining whether the provisions of this chapter are being complied with" (§13.04.310) Verified3.

State statute. An inspection warrant is "signed by a judge of a court of record" Verified4, and the application must say "consent to inspect has been sought and refused" or justify not asking Verified5.

California Constitution. Article I, §13 protects against "unreasonable seizures and searches" Verified6. "In the absence of consent or exigent circumstances, government officials engaged in the abatement of a public nuisance must have a warrant to enter any private property where such entry would invade a constitutionally protected privacy interest" (Gleaves v. Waters) Verified7.

U.S. Constitution. The Fourth Amendment, incorporated through the Fourteenth (Mapp v. Ohio, 367 U.S. 643 (1961)) Verified8. Inspections "are significant intrusions upon the interests protected by the Fourth Amendment" (Camara) Verified9.

Our reading: On its face the clause is silent about warrants; a court could read it as entry by consent or warrant. The as-applied question is what happens when someone says no. Full analysis: How it ties to Williams.

Example 2: sewer fees

Code. "Any other fees the city may deem necessary to carry out this chapter, established from time to time by the city council" (§13.08.040(E)) Verified10.

State statute. Notice of an increase "may be given by including it in the agency's regular billing statement" (Gov. Code §53755) Verified11.

California Constitution. Article XIII D, §6 requires mailed notice, a hearing, and no fee if a majority of owners protest; it names "sewer, water, and refuse collection services" Verified6. See XIII D.

U.S. Constitution. No direct rule on local fee votes. Due process requires notice "reasonably calculated" to reach people in some settings (Mullane v. Central Hanover Bank & Trust Co., 339 U.S. 306 (1950)) Verified12.

Our reading: The section is a delegation, not a fee. The question is whether each actual sewer fee went through XIII D, §6. Records that would show it: the mailed notice, the protest count, and the rate study.
