Incorporation: how the Bill of Rights came to bind the City of Williams

Lesson 30 of 68 · Equal treatment and due process · 8 min read

The Bill of Rights was written to limit Congress, not states or cities. This lesson shows how the Fourteenth Amendment changed that, one right at a time, and how a resident sues a city that violates one.

Key takeaways

  • In 1833 the Bill of Rights limited only the federal government (Barron).
  • The Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause now applies almost all of it to states (Timbs).
  • A city is part of the state, so incorporated rights bind Williams.
  • People sue cities for violations under 42 U.S.C. §1983, but only for the city's own policy or custom (Monell).

Before 1868: Barron v. Baltimore

The Fifth Amendment's takings rule "is intended solely as a limitation on the exercise of power by the government of the United States, and is not applicable to the legislation of the states" (Barron v. Baltimore, 32 U.S. 243 (1833)) Verified1. So in 1833 a city could take property without paying, as far as the federal Constitution was concerned.

The Fourteenth Amendment

Full lesson: Fourteenth Amendment.

How the Court decided, right by right

  • 1925, Gitlow: "we may and do assume that freedom of speech and of the press ... are among the fundamental personal rights and 'liberties' protected by the due process clause of the Fourteenth Amendment from impairment by the States" (Gitlow v. New York, 268 U.S. 652 (1925)) Verified3.
  • 1937, Palko: rights "implicit in the concept of ordered liberty" apply to the states (Palko v. Connecticut, 302 U.S. 319 (1937)) Verified4.
  • 1968, Duncan: "trial by jury in criminal cases is fundamental to the American scheme of justice" (Duncan v. Louisiana, 391 U.S. 145 (1968)) Verified5.
  • 2010, McDonald: the Fourteenth Amendment "makes the Second Amendment right to keep and bear arms fully applicable to the States" (McDonald v. Chicago, 561 U.S. 742 (2010)) Verified6.
  • 2019, Timbs: "With only 'a handful' of exceptions, this Court has held that the Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause incorporates the protections contained in the Bill of Rights" (Timbs v. Indiana, 586 U.S. 146 (2019)) Verified7.

Our reading: the gaps rarely matter in Williams, because California's constitution supplies its own versions, such as the civil jury right in Article I, §16 Verified8.

Why a city counts as "the State"

"Municipal corporations are political subdivisions of the state, created as convenient agencies for exercising such of the governmental powers of the state as may be intrusted to them" (Hunter v. City of Pittsburgh, 207 U.S. 161 (1907)) Verified9. When the Williams council passes an ordinance or an inspector knocks, the state is acting through them.

Suing a city: §1983 and Monell

"Local governing bodies (and local officials sued in their official capacities) can, therefore, be sued directly under § 1983 for monetary, declaratory, and injunctive relief in those situations where, as here, the action that is alleged to be unconstitutional implements or executes a policy statement, ordinance, regulation, or decision officially adopted" (Monell v. Department of Social Services, 436 U.S. 658 (1978)) Verified10. A state itself is not a "person" under §1983 (Will v. Michigan Dept. of State Police, 491 U.S. 58 (1989)) Verified11; a city is.

Our reading: an ordinance is official policy by definition. A claim that a Williams ordinance itself violates a right is the clearest kind of §1983 claim against the city; a claim about one employee's act needs more.

Check yourself

Pick an answer for each question. You will see right away if it is right, and why.

Question 1. Did the Bill of Rights bind states in 1833?

Show the answer and why

No (Barron v. Baltimore) Verified1.

Question 2. Which clause carries the Bill of Rights to the states?

Show the answer and why

The Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause (Timbs) Verified7.

Question 3. When can a resident sue the City of Williams under §1983?

Show the answer and why

When the violation comes from city policy, like an ordinance, or custom (Monell) Verified10.

Question 4. Scenario: Suppose a written city policy violates a resident's free-speech rights. Can the resident sue the city?

Show the answer and why

Answer: Yes, under §1983, for harm from the city's own policy or custom.
