Article I, Sections 1–7: How Congress is built and how a bill becomes law

Lesson 44 of 68 · Congress and federal power · 22 min read

Article I is the longest article, and it comes first because the framers saw the legislature as the center of the new government. Sections 1–7 build Congress and set the only path a federal law can take. Section 8 (next lesson) lists what Congress may regulate; Sections 9 and 10 list what Congress and the states may not do.

Key takeaways

  • Congress has only the legislative powers "herein granted." Everything else stays with the states or the people.
  • The House is elected by population; the Senate gives each state two votes. The original Senate was chosen by state legislatures until the Seventeenth Amendment.
  • A bill becomes law only by passing both houses and going to the President. The Court has struck down shortcuts around that path.
  • None of this structure binds California or Williams directly. It's a model, not a rule, for local government.

Section 1: All legislative power goes to Congress

What it says. Congress, made of a Senate and a House, holds "All legislative Powers herein granted." Two words carry the weight. "Herein granted" means Congress has only the powers the Constitution gives it. "All" means no one else in the federal government can make law.

Why it's there. Under the Articles of Confederation, Congress was a single body where each state had one vote. At the Convention, large states wanted representation by population, small states wanted equal votes. The deal that settled it, usually called the Great Compromise, produced a House by population and a Senate with equal representation for each state Verified2.

How courts read it. Because Congress holds the legislative power, the Court has struck down attempts to make law outside Congress's procedure, such as the one-house veto and the line-item veto. Both cases are in Section 7 below.

Section 2: The House of Representatives

Changed. Clause 3: Changed. The Archives notes that Article I, Section 2 "was modified by section 2 of the 14th amendment." The "three fifths of all other Persons" rule is dead; Section 2 of the Fourteenth Amendment counts "the whole number of persons in each State." Verified1,3

Clause by clause

  • Clause 1, elections every two years. House members serve two-year terms, and anyone qualified to vote for the larger house of their state legislature can vote for the House. Later amendments (15th, 19th, 24th, 26th) stopped states from denying the vote by race, sex, poll tax or age 18+.
  • Clause 2, qualifications. At least 25 years old, a citizen for seven years, and living in the state when elected. The Constitution doesn't require living in the district.
  • Clause 3, apportionment and the census. Seats are divided among the states by population, counted every ten years. The original formula counted enslaved people as "three fifths of all other Persons." That's dead text: the Fourteenth Amendment counts "the whole number of persons in each State" Verified3. The clause also caps the House at one member per 30,000 people, a floor that stopped mattering long ago.
  • Clause 4, vacancies. When a seat opens, the governor calls a special election ("Writs of Election"). Governors can't appoint House members.
  • Clause 5, Speaker and impeachment. The House chooses its Speaker and has "the sole Power of Impeachment," meaning the power to charge an official. The Senate holds the trial (Section 3).

How courts read it

  • Qualifications are fixed. In 1967 the House refused to seat Adam Clayton Powell, Jr., after he was elected. The Court held that since he "was duly elected by the voters" and "was not ineligible to serve under any provision of the Constitution," the House had no power to exclude him (Powell v. McCormack, 395 U.S. 486 (1969)) Verified4. In 1995 the Court held that states can't add qualifications either, striking down an Arkansas term-limit rule for members of Congress (U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton, 514 U.S. 779 (1995)) Verified5.
  • Equal districts. Clause 1 says Representatives are chosen "by the People." The Court held that this means "as nearly as is practicable one man's vote in a congressional election is to be worth as much as another's" (Wesberry v. Sanders, 376 U.S. 1 (1964)) Verified6. That is the origin of equal-population congressional districts. The same idea reaches state and local districts through the Fourteenth Amendment (Amendment 14).

Section 3: The Senate

Changed. Clause 1: Changed. The Archives notes that Article I, Section 3 "was modified by the 17th amendment." Senators are no longer "chosen by the Legislature"; since 1913 they are "elected by the people." Verified1,3

Changed. Clause 2: Changed. The vacancy rule in this clause (temporary appointments by the governor while the legislature is in recess) was replaced by the Seventeenth Amendment's vacancy paragraph, part of the change the Archives describes as Article I, Section 3 being "modified by the 17th amendment." Verified1,3

Clause by clause

  • Clause 1, two senators per state. Originally "chosen by the Legislature" of each state for six-year terms. Since the Seventeenth Amendment (1913), senators are "elected by the people" Verified3.
  • Clause 2, staggered terms. The Senate was split into three classes, so one-third is elected every two years. That keeps the Senate from turning over all at once. The vacancy rule at the end of this clause was replaced by the Seventeenth Amendment.
  • Clause 3, qualifications. At least 30, a citizen for nine years, living in the state when elected.
  • Clause 4, the Vice President. Presides over the Senate and votes only to break a tie.
  • Clause 5, officers. The Senate chooses a President pro tempore to preside when the Vice President is absent.
  • Clause 6, impeachment trials. The Senate has "the sole Power to try all Impeachments." Senators are under oath; the Chief Justice presides when the President is tried; conviction takes two-thirds of members present.
  • Clause 7, the limit on punishment. Conviction can remove a person from office and bar them from future federal office, nothing more. Criminal punishment, if any, comes separately in the regular courts.

How courts read it. When the Senate used a committee to hear evidence against impeached federal judge Walter Nixon, he argued that "try" required the full Senate. The Court held his claim "nonjusticiable": the Constitution commits impeachment trials to the Senate, and courts won't second-guess how it runs them (Nixon v. United States, 506 U.S. 224 (1993)) Verified7. Don't confuse this case with United States v. Nixon (1974), the Watergate tapes case covered in Article II.

Section 4: Elections and meetings

Changed. Clause 2: Changed. The Archives notes that Article I, Section 4 "was modified by section 2" of the Twentieth Amendment. Congress now meets "at noon on the 3d day of January," not the first Monday in December. Verified1,3

What it says. States set "the Times, Places and Manner" of congressional elections, but Congress can override most of those rules. Congress must meet at least once a year. The original meeting date (first Monday in December) was replaced by the Twentieth Amendment's January 3 Verified3.

Why it matters locally. This clause is why your ballot for Congress is printed, mailed and counted by the county under California's Elections Code, under federal rules Congress has added on top (registration, overseas voters, voting-rights protections). In Colusa County, the county elections office runs those elections, and city elections in Williams are typically consolidated with them Unverified8.

Section 5: Each house runs itself

Clause by clause. Each house judges its own members' elections and qualifications. A majority is a quorum to do business. Each house makes its own rules, can punish members for disorderly behavior, and can expel a member by two-thirds. Each keeps and publishes a journal of its proceedings, and one-fifth of members present can force a recorded yes-no vote. Neither house can adjourn for more than three days, or move elsewhere, without the other's consent.

How courts read it. "Judge of the ... Qualifications" doesn't let a house invent new qualifications. That was the point of Powell: the House can judge whether a member meets the Constitution's age, citizenship and residence rules, but it can't add a test of its own Verified4. Expulsion, by two-thirds, is different: it punishes a sitting member and is left to the house.

The local parallel. A city council also judges its own procedure, but not freely. California's Brown Act, not the council's preference, decides when the Williams council may meet in private, and requires public agendas and recorded votes. See the Brown Act lesson.

Section 6: Pay, privileges and the ban on double offices

Clause by clause

  • Pay. Members are paid "out of the Treasury of the United States," not by their states.
  • Privilege from arrest. Protects members going to and from sessions, except for "Treason, Felony and Breach of the Peace." In practice it covers only civil arrest, which barely exists today.
  • Speech or Debate. Members can't be "questioned in any other Place" for legislative acts. That shields votes, speeches and committee work from lawsuits and prosecution.
  • Incompatibility. No one can hold a federal executive or judicial office and sit in Congress at the same time. This is a key reason the U.S. has no parliamentary cabinet.
  • Emoluments for new offices. A member can't be appointed to an office created, or whose pay was raised, during that member's term.

How courts read it. The Speech or Debate Clause covers aides too. The Court held that a Senator's aides must be "treated as the latter's alter egos" for legislative acts, but the clause doesn't protect conduct outside the legislative process (Gravel v. United States, 408 U.S. 606 (1972)) Verified9.

The local parallel. City council members in California don't get Speech or Debate immunity from the U.S. Constitution. They are covered by state immunity statutes and by federal doctrines on legislative immunity in civil-rights suits, which are outside this course.

Section 7: How a bill becomes law

Clause by clause

  • Clause 1, revenue bills start in the House. Bills "for raising Revenue" must originate in the House, the chamber closest to the voters. The Senate can amend them.
  • Clause 2, presentment and veto. Every bill that passes both houses goes to the President. The President signs it, or returns it with objections (a veto). Two-thirds of each house can override. If the President does nothing for ten days (Sundays excepted), it becomes law, unless Congress has adjourned and prevented its return, in which case it dies (the "pocket veto").
  • Clause 3, orders and resolutions too. Any "Order, Resolution, or Vote" that needs both houses must also go to the President, except a vote to adjourn. This closes a loophole: Congress can't avoid the veto by calling a law something else.

How courts read it

  • No one-house veto. For decades Congress wrote laws letting one house cancel an agency's decision. The Court struck that down: the Constitution's lawmaking steps reflect "a single, finely wrought and exhaustively considered, procedure" (INS v. Chadha, 462 U.S. 919 (1983)) Verified10. Any action that changes legal rights must pass both houses and go to the President.
  • No line-item veto. In 1996 Congress let the President cancel individual spending items after signing a bill. The Court held that this produced laws that were not "the product of the 'finely wrought' procedure that the Framers designed," and struck it down (Clinton v. City of New York, 524 U.S. 417 (1998)) Verified11. The City of New York was a party because a cancelled item would have cost it Medicaid money.

How it reaches Williams

Sections 1–7 bind only the federal government. Nothing in them requires California or Williams to have two houses, a veto or any particular procedure. California has its own design in Article IV, and Williams, a general law city, follows the Government Code Verified12. Two pieces do reach you: Section 2's "by the People," which led to equal-population districts, and Section 4, which governs the congressional races on your ballot.

In Williams

  • The city council is a small legislature with no second house and no veto. In a general law city like Williams, ordinances are adopted by the council under state statutes, not under Article I. Ask how an ordinance was adopted: the Government Code, not the Constitution, sets the readings and publication rules (see How it fits: statutes).
  • The "who decides" question starts here. Article I teaches the habit of asking which body holds a power and how it must act. Use the same habit on Williams: is a fee set by ordinance, by resolution, or by a staff schedule? See Who decides?.

Common misconceptions

  • "Congress can pass a law on anything important." No. It has only the powers "herein granted." The list is in Section 8.
  • "The Constitution still says enslaved people count as three-fifths." The words are still in the original text, but the Fourteenth Amendment replaced them in 1868 Verified3.
  • "Senators were always elected by voters." Not until the Seventeenth Amendment in 1913 Verified3.
  • "Impeachment means removal." Impeachment is the House's charge. Removal takes a two-thirds Senate conviction.
  • "Members of Congress must live in their district." The Constitution only requires living in the state.

Check yourself

Answer in your head first, then open each question.

The House voted to exclude a member-elect who met the age, citizenship and residence rules. Can it do that?

No. In Powell v. McCormack the Court held the House could not exclude Adam Clayton Powell, Jr., who "was not ineligible to serve under any provision of the Constitution" Verified4.

Congress passes a law letting the House alone cancel an agency rule. What's wrong with it?

It skips bicameralism and presentment. INS v. Chadha struck down the one-house veto because lawmaking must follow "a single, finely wrought and exhaustively considered, procedure" Verified10.

What does Section 2's "chosen ... by the People" have to do with district lines?

Wesberry v. Sanders read it to require that "as nearly as is practicable one man's vote in a congressional election is to be worth as much as another's," which means equal-population districts Verified6.

Does Article I require the Williams City Council to have a mayoral veto?

No. Article I binds only the federal government. Williams follows California's Government Code as a general law city Verified12.
