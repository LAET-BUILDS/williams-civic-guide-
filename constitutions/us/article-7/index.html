Article VII: Ratification, and how the Constitution took effect

Lesson 66 of 68 · Changing the constitutions · 6 min read

On this page (9 sections)

Article VII says how the Constitution itself would become law: ratification by special conventions in nine of the thirteen states. It's the shortest article and finished its work in 1788–89, but it shows something important about the Constitution's authority.

Key takeaways

  • Nine state conventions were enough, not all thirteen as the Articles of Confederation required for amendments.
  • Conventions, not state legislatures, ratified. That tied the Constitution to the people, matching "We the People."
  • New Hampshire was the ninth state, in June 1788. The new government began operating in March 1789.
  • In Williams: the same idea, that the people approve their constitution, is how the California Constitution changes today. Every state constitutional amendment goes to the voters, including voters in Williams (Art. XVIII, §4).

The text

What it says

  • Nine of thirteen. Ratification by conventions of nine states was "sufficient for the Establishment of this Constitution between the States so ratifying the Same." A state that didn't ratify wasn't bound.
  • The note about corrections. The second paragraph lists words that were inserted between lines or written over erasures on the parchment, so no one could later claim the document was altered.
  • The attestation. Signed in Convention on September 17, 1787, attested by the Convention's secretary, William Jackson, followed by the signatures of the delegates Verified1.

History

The Articles of Confederation required every state to agree to any change. The framers went around that rule: they wrote a new constitution and sent it to state conventions elected for that purpose. Using conventions instead of state legislatures meant the Constitution came from the people of each state, the point made in the Preamble and later in McCulloch v. Maryland Verified2.

The count reached nine in the summer of 1788: "In June 1788, New Hampshire became the ninth state to ratify the Constitution, giving it effect in the ratifying states" Verified3. Our reading: Article VII's limit to the states "so ratifying" mattered here. A state that hadn't ratified wasn't part of the new arrangement until it did.

When did it take effect?

The ninth ratification was reached in 1788. The Supreme Court later explained the timing: Congress under the old Articles set the first Wednesday in March 1789 as the start, and "its operation did not commence before the first Wednesday in March 1789" (Owings v. Speed, 1820, as quoted in the Constitution Annotated) Verified4.

The same case explains why the old and new governments couldn't overlap: "Both Governments could not be understood to exist at the same time. The New Government did not commence until the old Government expired" Verified4.

How it reaches Williams

It doesn't today; its job is done. California joined the Union decades after ratification and was bound by the Constitution as a new state under Article IV.

In Williams

Article VII is history, but its main idea is alive in California. The U.S. Constitution took effect only after the people of the states approved it in conventions. California goes further for its own constitution: every change goes straight to the voters.

"A proposed amendment or revision shall be submitted to the electors and, if approved by a majority of votes cast thereon, takes effect on the fifth day after the Secretary of State files the statement of the vote for the election at which the measure is voted on" (Cal. Const., Art. XVIII, §4) Verified5.

Our reading: when a state constitutional amendment is on your ballot in Williams, you're doing in a small way what the ratifying conventions did in 1787–88: deciding whether a change becomes part of the constitution. Changes to the U.S. Constitution work differently. Voters don't vote on them directly; see Article V. For California's process, see Article XVIII.

Common misconceptions

  • "All thirteen states had to agree." Nine was enough.
  • "State legislatures ratified the Constitution." Special conventions did.
  • "It took effect when it was signed in 1787." It was signed on September 17, 1787, but the Supreme Court said its operation "did not commence before the first Wednesday in March 1789" Verified4.

Check yourself

Pick an answer for each question. You will see right away if it is right, and why.

Question 1. How many state conventions were needed to establish the Constitution?

Show the answer and why

Nine Verified1.

Question 2. Why did the framers use conventions instead of state legislatures?

Show the answer and why

To ground the Constitution in the people of each state, consistent with "We the People" and McCulloch's reading that the government "proceeds directly from the people" Verified2.

Question 3. Which state was the ninth to ratify, and when?

Show the answer and why

New Hampshire, in June 1788 Verified3.

Question 4. How does a change to the California Constitution take effect?

Show the answer and why

It goes to the voters and needs "a majority of votes cast thereon" (Art. XVIII, §4) Verified5.

Question 5. Scenario: Suppose a change to the California Constitution is proposed. Who must approve it?

Show the answer and why

Answer: The voters, including voters in Williams.

The same idea behind Article VII, that the people approve their constitution, is how the California Constitution changes today: every state constitutional amendment goes to the voters.
