Twelfth Amendment: Separate electoral votes for President and Vice President

Lesson 51 of 68 · The President, federal courts and the states · 7 min read

On this page (8 sections)

The original Constitution had each elector cast two votes for President, with the runner-up becoming Vice President. That broke in 1800. The Twelfth Amendment fixed it by having electors vote separately for each office.

Key takeaways

  • Passed by Congress December 9, 1803; ratified June 15, 1804. It superseded part of Article II, §1, and part of it was later superseded by the Twentieth Amendment, §3 Verified1.
  • Electors cast one vote for President and a separate vote for Vice President.
  • If no one wins a majority, the House picks the President (one vote per state delegation) and the Senate picks the Vice President.
  • States can bind electors to vote for their party's nominee (Chiafalo, 2020).

The text

Changed. Partly superseded. The bracketed sentence marked with an asterisk was "Superseded by section 3 of the 20th amendment," per the Archives. Verified2,1

History

"Problems arose under the original system in the election of 1800, when Thomas Jefferson and Aaron Burr received the same number of votes in the Electoral College, sending the selection of a President to the House of Representatives, despite the fact that the electors had intended Jefferson to be President and Burr to be Vice President" Verified3. The amendment "was designed to avoid a repetition of the events of 1800 by having the electors vote separately for President and Vice President" Verified3.

Clause by clause

  • Electors meet in their own states and vote by ballot for President and Vice President, at least one of whom must not be from the elector's own state.
  • Lists go to the President of the Senate, who opens them before both houses.
  • Majority of electors appointed wins. Otherwise the House chooses from the top three, voting by state, "the representation from each state having one vote."
  • Vice President. If no majority, the Senate chooses from the top two.
  • Eligibility. No one ineligible to be President can be Vice President.
  • Superseded part. The sentence about the House choosing "before the fourth day of March" was superseded by the Twentieth Amendment, §3 Verified1.

How courts read it

  • Ray v. Blair (1952). A state party can require would-be electors to pledge to support the party's nominee (Ray v. Blair, 343 U.S. 214 (1952)) Verified4.
  • Chiafalo v. Washington (2020). "A State may enforce an elector's pledge to support his party's nominee—and the state voters' choice—for President" (Chiafalo v. Washington, 591 U.S. 578 (2020)) Verified5. Washington had "fined the Electors $1,000 apiece for breaking their pledges" in 2016 Verified5.

In Williams

No direct effect on city government. Williams voters choose presidential electors through the statewide California ballot, administered locally by Colusa County Verified6. California's own election rules are in Article II.

Common misconceptions

  • "Electors are free agents." States can require and enforce pledges Verified5.
  • "The House votes by member in a contingent election." It votes by state delegation, one vote per state.

Check yourself

Pick an answer for each question. You will see right away if it is right, and why.

Question 1. What happened in 1800 that led to the Twelfth Amendment?

Show the answer and why

Jefferson and Burr tied in electoral votes, throwing the election to the House even though electors meant Jefferson for President Verified3.

Question 2. In a House contingent election, how many votes does California get?

Show the answer and why

One. Each state's delegation casts a single vote.

Question 3. Can a state punish an elector who breaks a pledge?

Show the answer and why

Yes. Chiafalo v. Washington (2020) Verified5.

Question 4. Match who decides: Match each situation to who chooses.

Show the answer and why
  • No candidate for President wins an electoral-vote majority: The House, one vote per state delegation
  • No candidate for Vice President wins a majority: The Senate
  • Normal year: President and Vice President: Electors, with separate votes for each
