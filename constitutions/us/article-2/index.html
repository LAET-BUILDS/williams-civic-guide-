Article II: The President and the executive branch

Lesson 50 of 68 · The President, federal courts and the states · 18 min read

Article II creates one President who holds "The executive Power." It's shorter and vaguer than Article I, and most fights over presidential power come from that vagueness. This lesson goes section by section, then shows the four cases that frame modern presidential power: Youngstown, United States v. Nixon, the removal cases, and Trump v. United States.

Key takeaways

  • The President is elected indirectly, by electors chosen in each state in the way its legislature directs.
  • The President commands the military, grants federal pardons, makes treaties (with two-thirds of the Senate) and appoints officers (with Senate consent).
  • The President must "take Care that the Laws be faithfully executed": enforce the laws Congress passed, not only the ones the President likes.
  • Article II doesn't apply to governors or city managers. California sets up its executive in its own Article V.

Section 1: Choosing the President

Changed. Clause 3: Superseded. The Archives notes that "A portion of Article II, section 1 of the Constitution was superseded by the 12th amendment." This is the original electoral-vote clause; electors now cast separate ballots for President and Vice President. Verified1,2

Changed. Clause 6: Affected. The Archives notes that "Article II, section 1, of the Constitution was affected by the 25th amendment," which now governs presidential vacancy and disability. Verified1,2

Clause by clause

  • Clause 1, the vesting clause. "The executive Power shall be vested in a President." One person, not a council, a choice the framers debated. Four-year term, with a Vice President.
  • Clause 2, electors. Each state appoints electors "in such Manner as the Legislature thereof may direct," equal to its total seats in Congress. No member of Congress or federal officer can be an elector. Every state now chooses electors by popular vote, but that's state law, not a constitutional requirement.
  • Clause 3, the original voting system (superseded). Each elector cast two votes; the runner-up became Vice President. After the tied election of 1800, the Twelfth Amendment replaced this clause Verified2.
  • Clause 4, timing. Congress sets the day electors are chosen and the day they vote, the same across the country.
  • Clause 5, qualifications. A "natural born Citizen," at least 35, and 14 years a resident of the United States.
  • Clause 6, succession. If the President dies, resigns or can't serve, the Vice President takes over the powers; Congress handles what happens beyond that. The Twenty-fifth Amendment now fills in the details Verified2.
  • Clause 7, salary. Can't be raised or cut during the term, and no other federal or state pay.
  • Clause 8, the oath. "I do solemnly swear (or affirm) that I will faithfully execute the Office of President of the United States, and will to the best of my Ability, preserve, protect and defend the Constitution of the United States." The "(or affirm)" option exists so no one has to swear a religious oath.

How courts read it. States control their electors. In 2020 the Court held that "A State may enforce an elector's pledge to support his party's nominee—and the state voters' choice—for President" (Chiafalo v. Washington, 591 U.S. 578 (2020)) Verified3. The Court relied on clause 2's grant to the state legislature.

Section 2: Powers

Clause by clause

  • Clause 1, Commander in Chief, opinions, pardons. The President commands the armed forces and the militia when called into federal service; can require written opinions from department heads; and can grant "Reprieves and Pardons for Offences against the United States, except in Cases of Impeachment." A presidential pardon covers only federal crimes. It can't touch a California conviction or a Williams infraction.
  • Clause 2, treaties and appointments. Treaties need two-thirds of the Senators present. The President nominates and, with the Senate's "Advice and Consent," appoints ambassadors, Supreme Court justices and other officers. Congress can let the President alone, the courts, or department heads appoint "inferior Officers."
  • Clause 3, recess appointments. When the Senate is in recess, the President can fill vacancies with commissions that expire at the end of the next session. The Court held that "a recess of more than 3 days but less than 10 days is presumptively too short" to trigger this power (NLRB v. Noel Canning, 573 U.S. 513 (2014)) Verified4.

Section 3: Duties

The President reports to Congress "from time to time" on the State of the Union, recommends legislation, can call Congress into special session, receives ambassadors, commissions officers, and "shall take Care that the Laws be faithfully executed." That last phrase is a duty, not a grant of open-ended power: the President carries out laws Congress made.

Section 4: Impeachment

The President, Vice President and "all civil Officers" are removed on impeachment and conviction for "Treason, Bribery, or other high Crimes and Misdemeanors." The House impeaches; the Senate tries (Article I, Sections 2 and 3). The Court treats the Senate's trial procedures as beyond judicial review (Nixon v. United States, 1993) Verified1,5.

How courts read it

1. The three-zone test (1952). During the Korean War, President Truman seized the nation's steel mills to prevent a strike. The Court held the seizure unconstitutional: no statute authorized it, and Article II didn't. Justice Jackson's concurrence became the standard framework (Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer, 343 U.S. 579 (1952)) Verified6:

  • "When the President acts pursuant to an express or implied authorization of Congress, his authority is at its maximum."
  • In between, when Congress is silent, there's a "zone of twilight."
  • "When the President takes measures incompatible with the expressed or implied will of Congress, his power is at its lowest ebb."

2. No absolute privilege (1974). President Nixon refused to hand over White House tapes subpoenaed for a criminal trial. The Court held unanimously that "Neither the doctrine of separation of powers nor the generalized need for confidentiality of high-level communications, without more, can sustain an absolute unqualified presidential privilege of immunity from judicial process under all circumstances" (United States v. Nixon, 418 U.S. 683 (1974)) Verified7. He released the tapes and resigned weeks later.

3. Removal of officers. The Constitution says how officers are appointed but not how they're removed. The Court held in 1926 that the President can fire executive officers he appoints (Myers v. United States, 272 U.S. 52 (1926)) Verified8. In 1935 it carved out multi-member agencies whose duties were "predominantly quasi judicial and quasi legislative" (Humphrey's Executor v. United States, 295 U.S. 602 (1935)) Verified9. In 1988 it upheld the independent counsel (Morrison v. Olson, 487 U.S. 654 (1988)) Verified10. In 2020 it held that the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's "leadership by a single individual removable only for inefficiency, neglect, or malfeasance violates the separation of powers" (Seila Law LLC v. Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, 591 U.S. 197 (2020)) Verified11.

4. Immunity from prosecution (2024). The Court held that "a former President [has] some immunity from criminal prosecution for official acts during his tenure in office," absolute for "core constitutional powers," at least presumptive for other official acts, and "There is no immunity for unofficial acts" (Trump v. United States, 603 U.S. 593 (2024)) Verified12.

How it reaches Williams

Article II binds only the federal executive. It doesn't limit the city manager or the council, and a city's executive structure comes from California law. Two pieces still touch you:

  • Federal enforcement in the city. Federal agencies enforce federal law in Williams under the President's direction.
  • Pardons stop at the federal line. Only the Governor can pardon a California conviction (Cal. Const., Art. V, §8) Verified13.

In Williams

  • The oath idea carries down. Every city official in California takes an oath to support both constitutions; the text is in California's Article XX, §3 Verified13. See Article XX.
  • "Take care" has a local version. A city's staff carry out ordinances the council passed. Our reading: a city employee can't refuse to apply a state law because he thinks it's unconstitutional; the California Supreme Court held local officials have no such power (Lockyer v. City and County of San Francisco) Verified14.
  • The Youngstown habit. When a city official acts, ask: what ordinance or statute authorizes this? Power is at its lowest when it contradicts what the council or the Legislature said.

Common misconceptions

  • "Voters elect the President directly." Voters choose electors under state law; electors vote for President Verified3.
  • "The President can pardon anyone for anything." Only "Offences against the United States," and not impeachment.
  • "Executive privilege is absolute." United States v. Nixon says it isn't Verified7.
  • "The President can ignore a law he disagrees with." He must "take Care that the Laws be faithfully executed," and Youngstown puts his power at its "lowest ebb" when he acts against Congress Verified6.

Check yourself

Pick an answer for each question. You will see right away if it is right, and why.

Question 1. Can a President's pardon wipe out a California misdemeanor conviction?

Show the answer and why

No. The pardon power covers only "Offences against the United States." California pardons belong to the Governor (Cal. Const., Art. V, §8) Verified1,13.

Question 2. What are Justice Jackson's three zones in Youngstown?

Show the answer and why

Maximum authority when Congress authorizes; a "zone of twilight" when Congress is silent; "lowest ebb" when the President acts against Congress's will Verified6.

Question 3. Can a state punish an elector who votes against the candidate who won the state's popular vote?

Show the answer and why

Yes. Chiafalo v. Washington: "A State may enforce an elector's pledge" Verified3.

Question 4. Does Article II apply to the Williams city manager?

Show the answer and why

No. It binds the federal executive only; the city's structure comes from California law.

Question 5. Match who decides: Match each power to who holds it.

Show the answer and why
  • Pardoning a federal crime: The President
  • Pardoning a California conviction: The Governor of California
  • Deciding how a state's electors are chosen: Each state's legislature
  • Making a treaty: The President, with two-thirds of the Senate

A presidential pardon covers only federal crimes; only the Governor can pardon a California conviction. Each state appoints electors as its legislature directs. The President makes treaties with two-thirds of the Senate.
