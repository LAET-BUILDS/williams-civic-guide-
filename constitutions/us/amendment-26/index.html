Twenty-sixth Amendment: Voting at 18

Lesson 6 of 68 · Voting and elections · 7 min read

On this page (8 sections)

  2. What it says
  3. History
  4. How courts read it
  5. In Williams
  6. Common misconceptions
  7. Check yourself
  8. Sources cited on this page

The Twenty-sixth Amendment says citizens 18 and older can't be denied the vote "on account of age." It took about three months to ratify, after the Supreme Court split on whether Congress could lower the voting age by statute. It applies to every election, Williams City Council races included. In California, 16- and 17-year-olds can preregister so they're ready to vote at 18.

Key takeaways

  • Passed by Congress March 23, 1971; ratified July 1, 1971. It replaced the "twenty-one" in Fourteenth Amendment §2 Verified1.
  • It covers every election: federal, state and local.
  • It followed Oregon v. Mitchell (1970), which let Congress set 18 for federal elections but not state and local ones Verified2.
  • The California Supreme Court enforced it in 1971, ruling that 18-to-20-year-olds can register where they actually live Verified3.
  • In Williams: a 16- or 17-year-old can preregister, and anyone who will be 18 by the next election can register for it Verified4,5.

The text

What it says

  • "Citizens of the United States, who are eighteen years of age or older." It sets a floor. A state may not shut out voters 18 and older because of age Verified1.
  • "Shall not be denied or abridged." "Abridged" means weakened, not just denied. Courts have used it against rules that made voting harder for young people (see below).
  • "By the United States or by any State." The same words as the Fifteenth and Nineteenth Amendments, so it reaches local elections too Verified1.
  • Section 2. Congress may enforce it. Federal law lets the Justice Department sue state and local governments that violate it Verified3.

History

  • World War II. Congress lowered the draft age to 18. That led some officials to ask why 18- to 20-year-old servicemembers couldn't vote, and the slogan "old enough to fight, old enough to vote" caught on Verified3.
  • Korea and Vietnam. Support grew "as Americans questioned why young soldiers fighting in Korea and Vietnam could not exercise the franchise" Verified3.
  • 1970: a statute and a split Court. Congress "lowered the minimum voting age to 18 in federal elections—but not state elections—by enacting the Voting Rights Act Amendments of 1970" Verified3. The 1970 law tried to cover all elections. In Oregon v. Mitchell, the deciding opinion said Congress could "enfranchise 18-year-old citizens in national elections" but "cannot set the voting age in state and local elections" (Oregon v. Mitchell, 400 U.S. 112 (1970)) Verified2.
  • 1971: about three months. Congress sent the amendment to the states "on March 23, 1971," and it was ratified July 1 Verified3,1. President Nixon said the "11 million new young voters" would bring "a spirit of moral courage" to the electorate Verified3.

How courts read it

"The Supreme Court has not decided any cases definitively interpreting the Twenty-Sixth Amendment" Verified3. Other courts have:

  • Jolicoeur v. Mihaly (California Supreme Court, 1971). State officials "violated the Twenty-Sixth Amendment when they treated unmarried 'minor' citizens of at least 18 years of age who did not live with their parents 'differently from adults' for voting purposes." They had refused to let these young voters register where they actually lived Verified3. Our reading: a college student or young worker who has moved out can register at their own address, the same as any adult.
  • Texas residency rules. A federal court struck down rules that set voting residency for people under 21 "on a different basis than persons twenty-one years of age and older" Verified3.

In Williams

  • City elections are included. Any U.S. citizen 18 or older who lives in Williams and is registered can vote for City Council and on local measures. California's constitution says: "A United States citizen 18 years of age and resident in this State may vote" (Art. II, §2(a)) Verified6.
  • Preregister at 16. A person may preregister if they are a citizen, a California resident, not imprisoned for a felony, "and at least 16 years of age" (Elections Code §2101(b)) Verified4. To register to vote, you must be "at least 18 years of age at the time of the next election" (§2101(a)) Verified4.
  • Deadline. A registration counts if the county elections official gets it "on or before the 15th day before an election," including through the Secretary of State's website Verified5. Colusa County runs registration and elections for Williams Verified7.
  • Where you live counts. You vote where you reside, so a young adult who moved out of a parent's home in another town can register in Williams (see Jolicoeur above) Verified8,3.

Common misconceptions

  • "It only covers federal elections." That was the 1970 statute after Oregon v. Mitchell. The amendment covers every election Verified2,1.
  • "Students have to vote where their parents live." Jolicoeur rejected treating 18-to-20-year-olds differently from other adults on residence Verified3.
  • "You can't do anything until you turn 18." In California you can preregister at 16 Verified4.
  • "The amendment bans all age limits." It protects those 18 and older. It doesn't require letting younger people vote.

Check yourself

Pick an answer for each question. You will see right away if it is right, and why.

Question 1. What did Oregon v. Mitchell decide about the 1970 law?

Show the answer and why

Congress could set 18 as the voting age for federal elections but not for state and local elections Verified2.

Question 2. Does the Twenty-sixth Amendment apply to a Williams City Council election?

Show the answer and why

Yes. It binds the United States and every state, including in local elections Verified1.

Question 3. At what age can a Californian preregister to vote?

Show the answer and why

16 (Elections Code §2101(b)) Verified4.

Question 4. What did the California Supreme Court decide in Jolicoeur v. Mihaly?

Show the answer and why

Young adults 18 and older who live on their own must be allowed to register where they actually live, like other adults Verified3.

Question 5. Scenario: Suppose a 17-year-old Williams resident will turn 18 before the next city election. What can they do now?

Show the answer and why

Answer: Register now and vote in that election.

Anyone who will be 18 by the next election can register for it, and 16- and 17-year-olds can preregister.
