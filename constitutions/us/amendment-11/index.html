Eleventh Amendment: Suits against states

Lesson 22 of 68 · U.S. Constitution: Amendments 11–27 · 5 min read

On this page (8 sections)

The Eleventh Amendment overruled a 1793 Supreme Court decision that let a citizen of another state sue Georgia in federal court. Courts have since read it as part of a broader rule of state sovereign immunity. The key practical point for Williams residents: that immunity belongs to the state, not to cities or counties.

Key takeaways

  • Passed by Congress March 4, 1794; ratified February 7, 1795 Verified[1].
  • States generally can't be sued for money in federal court without their consent, even by their own citizens (Hans).
  • Officials can be sued to stop ongoing violations of federal law (Ex parte Young).
  • Cities and counties aren't protected (Lincoln County v. Luning; Mt. Healthy).

The text

Official text — Amendment XI · read in context

The Judicial power of the United States shall not be construed to extend to any suit in law or equity, commenced or prosecuted against one of the United States by Citizens of another State, or by Citizens or Subjects of any Foreign State.

Source: National Archives transcription Verified[1]

Why it's there

In Chisholm v. Georgia (1793), the Court let a South Carolina executor sue Georgia for a debt (Chisholm v. Georgia, 2 U.S. 419 (1793)) Verified[2]. States reacted quickly; Congress proposed the amendment the next year Verified[1]. The Archives notes that "Article III, section 2, of the Constitution was modified by amendment 11" Verified[1].

How courts read it

  • Own citizens too. The text covers suits by citizens of other states and foreign countries. Hans v. Louisiana (1890) acknowledged "the amendment does so read" but held a state also can't be sued by its own citizens in federal court (Hans v. Louisiana, 134 U.S. 1 (1890)) Verified[3].
  • State courts too. Congress can't subject nonconsenting states "to private suits for damages in state courts" under its Article I powers (Alden v. Maine, 527 U.S. 706 (1999)) Verified[4].
  • Suits against officials. An official enforcing an unconstitutional law "is in that case stripped of his official or representative character," so a federal court can order the official to stop (Ex parte Young, 209 U.S. 123 (1908)) Verified[5].
  • §1983. "A State is not a 'person'" under §1983 (Will v. Michigan Dept. of State Police, 491 U.S. 58 (1989)) Verified[6], so a damages suit under that statute can't name the State of California.

Cities and counties

Local governments don't share this immunity. In 1890 the Court rejected a county's argument that "because the county is an integral part of the state, it could not, under the eleventh amendment ... be sued": "The eleventh amendment limits the jurisdiction only as to suits against a state" (Lincoln County v. Luning, 133 U.S. 529 (1890)) Verified[7]. A local school board "is more like a county or city than it is an arm of the State" and "is not immune from suit under the Eleventh Amendment" (Mt. Healthy City School Dist. Bd. of Ed. v. Doyle, 429 U.S. 274 (1977)) Verified[8]. And cities are "persons" who can be sued under §1983 when an official policy causes a violation (Monell v. Department of Social Services, 436 U.S. 658 (1978)) Verified[9].

How it reaches Williams

It doesn't protect Williams. A resident can sue the City of Williams in federal court under §1983 for a constitutional violation caused by city policy Verified[7][9]. State-law claims against a California city are governed by the Government Claims Act's filing rules; this site hasn't verified the deadlines, so check them.

Common misconceptions

  • "You can't sue the government." You can't usually sue the state for damages in federal court. Cities are different Verified[7].
  • "The text only covers out-of-state plaintiffs." True of the text; Hans extended the rule Verified[3].

Check yourself

Answer in your head first, then open each question.

Can a Williams resident sue the State of California for damages under §1983?

No. Will v. Michigan Dept. of State Police held a state isn't a "person" under §1983 Verified[6].

Can the resident sue the City of Williams under §1983?

Yes, where an official city policy caused the violation. Cities have no Eleventh Amendment immunity and are "persons" under Monell Verified[7][9].

How can a federal court stop a state official from enforcing an unconstitutional law?

Through a suit against the official under Ex parte Young Verified[5].
