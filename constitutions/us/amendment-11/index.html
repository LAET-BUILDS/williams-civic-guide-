Eleventh Amendment: Suits against states

Lesson 57 of 68 · The President, federal courts and the states · 9 min read

The Eleventh Amendment overruled a 1793 Supreme Court decision that let a citizen of another state sue Georgia in federal court. Courts have since read it as part of a broader rule of state sovereign immunity. The key practical point for Williams residents: that immunity belongs to the state, not to cities or counties.

Key takeaways

  • Passed by Congress March 4, 1794; ratified February 7, 1795 Verified1.
  • States generally can't be sued for money in federal court without their consent, even by their own citizens (Hans).
  • Officials can be sued to stop ongoing violations of federal law (Ex parte Young).
  • Cities and counties aren't protected (Lincoln County v. Luning; Mt. Healthy).
  • In Williams: the city can be sued, but state-law money claims against it must first be presented in writing, often within six months (Gov. Code §911.2).

The text

History

In Chisholm v. Georgia (1793), the Court let a South Carolina executor sue Georgia for a debt (Chisholm v. Georgia, 2 U.S. 419 (1793)) Verified2. States reacted quickly; Congress proposed the amendment the next year Verified1. The Archives notes that "Article III, section 2, of the Constitution was modified by amendment 11" Verified1.

How courts read it

  • Own citizens too. The text covers suits by citizens of other states and foreign countries. Hans v. Louisiana (1890) acknowledged "the amendment does so read" but held a state also can't be sued by its own citizens in federal court (Hans v. Louisiana, 134 U.S. 1 (1890)) Verified3.
  • State courts too. Congress can't subject nonconsenting states "to private suits for damages in state courts" under its Article I powers (Alden v. Maine, 527 U.S. 706 (1999)) Verified4.
  • Suits against officials. An official enforcing an unconstitutional law "is in that case stripped of his official or representative character," so a federal court can order the official to stop (Ex parte Young, 209 U.S. 123 (1908)) Verified5.
  • §1983. "A State is not a 'person'" under §1983 (Will v. Michigan Dept. of State Police, 491 U.S. 58 (1989)) Verified6, so a damages suit under that statute can't name the State of California.

Cities and counties

Local governments don't share this immunity. In 1890 the Court rejected a county's argument that "because the county is an integral part of the state, it could not, under the eleventh amendment ... be sued": "The eleventh amendment limits the jurisdiction only as to suits against a state" (Lincoln County v. Luning, 133 U.S. 529 (1890)) Verified7. A local school board "is more like a county or city than it is an arm of the State" and "is not immune from suit under the Eleventh Amendment" (Mt. Healthy City School Dist. Bd. of Ed. v. Doyle, 429 U.S. 274 (1977)) Verified8. And cities are "persons" who can be sued under §1983 when an official policy causes a violation (Monell v. Department of Social Services, 436 U.S. 658 (1978)) Verified9.

How it reaches Williams

It doesn't protect Williams. A resident can sue the City of Williams in federal court under §1983 for a constitutional violation caused by city policy Verified7,9. State-law claims against a California city are governed by the Government Claims Act's filing rules (see below).

In Williams

The Eleventh Amendment protects the State of California. It doesn't protect the City of Williams Verified7. Two practical points follow.

  • Federal court. If a Williams city policy violates your federal constitutional rights, the city itself can be sued under §1983, but only for harm caused by "official municipal policy" (see the course overview) Verified10.
  • State-law claims have short deadlines. Before suing a public entity for money on most state-law claims, you first present a written claim. For "death or for injury to person or to personal property or growing crops," the claim is due "not later than six months after the accrual of the cause of action"; most other claims are due "not later than one year after the accrual of the cause of action" (Gov. Code §911.2) Verified11. If the entity sends written notice rejecting the claim, a lawsuit generally must be filed "not later than six months after the date such notice is personally delivered or deposited in the mail" (Gov. Code §945.6) Verified12.

Our reading: those deadlines have exceptions and details this page doesn't cover, and missing one can end a claim. If you think you have a claim against the city, talk to a licensed California attorney early. This site isn't legal advice.

Common misconceptions

  • "You can't sue the government." You can't usually sue the state for damages in federal court. Cities are different Verified7.
  • "The text only covers out-of-state plaintiffs." True of the text; Hans extended the rule Verified3.

Check yourself

Pick an answer for each question. You will see right away if it is right, and why.

Question 1. Can a Williams resident sue the State of California for damages under §1983?

Show the answer and why

No. Will v. Michigan Dept. of State Police held a state isn't a "person" under §1983 Verified6.

Question 2. Can the resident sue the City of Williams under §1983?

Show the answer and why

Yes, where an official city policy caused the violation. Cities have no Eleventh Amendment immunity and are "persons" under Monell Verified7,9.

Question 3. How can a federal court stop a state official from enforcing an unconstitutional law?

Show the answer and why

Through a suit against the official under Ex parte Young Verified5.

Question 4. Put the steps in order: Suppose a city truck damages your fence and you want money under state law. Put the steps in order.

Use the Up and Down buttons to change the order, then press Check.

  1. File a lawsuit, generally within six months of that notice
  2. The city sends written notice rejecting the claim
  3. Present a written claim to the city

Show the answer and why

The right order:

  1. Present a written claim to the city
  2. The city sends written notice rejecting the claim
  3. File a lawsuit, generally within six months of that notice

Before suing a public entity for money on most state-law claims, you first present a written claim. If the claim is rejected in writing, a lawsuit generally must be filed within six months of that notice. The deadlines have exceptions, so talk to a lawyer early.
