This page puts real Williams code sections next to the constitutional rules they touch. Every comparison gives you the code's exact words, the rule with its source, our reading, and the question a court would have to answer.

What this page does not do. It doesn't say any Williams section is unconstitutional. Only a court can decide that, usually on the facts of a real case, and we know of no court ruling on any of these Williams sections. A clause that looks risky on paper may be fine as the city uses it, and the reverse is also true. Code quotes come from a third-party copy on GoCodebook, current through Ordinance 275-25 (December 2025) Verified1. The official code is on Municode Verified2. Check a quote there or with the city clerk before you rely on it. For who holds each power, see Who decides?

1. Entry and inspection vs. the Fourth Amendment and Art. I, §13

The Williams text

"have the right of entering any or all premises for the purpose of determining whether the provisions of this chapter are being complied with." (§13.04.310, water) Verified6

"shall be permitted to enter all properties from which wastes or wastewaters are being or are capable of being discharged into the sewage system" (§13.08.060, sewer) Verified7

"If any water user refuses entry by, or hinders or prevents inspection by the director or his representative, then the city may discontinue water service to the premises until and unless entry is allowed." (§13.04.220(E)) Verified6

Compare the garbage chapter, which allows entry "after obtaining either a warrant or permission to do so from the owner or occupant of the premises" (§8.08.090) Verified8.

The constitutional rules

  • Inspections are searches. Housing-code inspections "are significant intrusions upon the interests protected by the Fourth Amendment," and without a warrant procedure they "lack the traditional safeguards which the Fourth Amendment guarantees to the individual." The tenant in that case "had a constitutional right to insist that the inspectors obtain a warrant" (Camara, 1967) Verified9.
  • A penalty for refusing is a red flag. The Court said "broad statutory safeguards are no substitute for individualized review, particularly when those safeguards may only be invoked at the risk of a criminal penalty" Verified9.
  • California says the same for nuisance abatement. "In the absence of consent or exigent circumstances, government officials engaged in the abatement of a public nuisance must have a warrant to enter any private property where such entry would invade a constitutionally protected privacy interest" (Gleaves v. Waters, 1985) Verified10. Note the limit: the court applied this only to entries that "infringe upon constitutionally recognized expectations of privacy" Verified10.
  • State law supplies the warrant. An inspection warrant is "signed by a judge of a court of record" Verified11. The application must say "consent to inspect has been sought and refused" or justify not asking Verified12. A warrant inspection "shall not be made by means of forcible entry" unless the judge expressly allows it, and after a refusal, notice "must be given at least 24 hours before the warrant is executed," unless the judge finds that immediate execution is needed Verified13.
  • Emergencies are different. The warrant rule doesn't "foreclose prompt inspections, even without a warrant, that the law has traditionally upheld in emergency situations" Verified9.

Our reading

The water and sewer clauses don't mention a warrant or consent, but courts often read a clause like that alongside the warrant rules rather than striking it down. A court could treat "right of entering" as entry by consent or warrant. The water shutoff for refusing entry (§13.04.220(E)) is the harder question, because it attaches a penalty to refusing. Camara involved a criminal penalty; a utility shutoff is a different kind of pressure. Whether that difference matters is a question no court has answered for Williams.

What would settle it: a court ruling in a real case, or the council adding §8.08.090's own words ("after obtaining either a warrant or permission") to the water and sewer chapters.

2. Daily fines vs. the excessive fines clauses

The Williams text

"Each person shall be guilty of a separate offense for each and every day during any portion of which any violation of any provision of the ordinances of the City of Williams is committed, continued or permitted by any such person" (§1.12.040) Verified14

As infractions the fines are $100, then $200, then $500 (§1.12.030) Verified14. Sewer administrative fines can reach $5,000 per violation per day (§13.08.930) Verified15.

The constitutional rules

  • Both constitutions forbid excessive fines, and the federal clause binds cities (Timbs, 2019) Verified16,3.
  • Test: a penalty is unconstitutional "if it is grossly disproportional to the gravity of the offense" (Bajakajian) Verified17. California courts weigh "(1) the defendant's culpability; (2) the relationship between the harm and the penalty; (3) the penalties imposed in similar statutes; and (4) the defendant's ability to pay" (R.J. Reynolds) Verified18.
  • Stacking daily penalties draws scrutiny. In Hale v. Morgan, $100 a day became $17,300, and the court called a "mandatory, fixed, substantial and cumulative punitive sanction" applied to people "of such disparate culpability" "manifestly suspect" Verified19.

Our reading

The rule in §1.12.040 (each day is a separate offense) is common and, by itself, isn't what courts test. Courts look at the total actually imposed on a particular person against the four factors above. So the question is never "is §1.12.040 valid?" but "is this total, for this person, for this harm, grossly disproportional?" Two points help a resident: ability to pay is a factor in California, and Hale shows daily totals can be challenged even when each day's amount is small.

What would settle it: a challenge to a specific total, on its own facts. What helps your case: proof you tried to fix the problem, proof of what you can afford, and proof of what others paid for the same thing (records to request).

3. Paying to be heard vs. due process

The Williams text

"together with either an advance deposit of the fine or an approved request for a deposit waiver." (§8.16.510(A)) Verified20

"demonstrating to the satisfaction of the city finance officer, or his or her designee, the person's actual financial inability to deposit" … "The written determination of the city finance officer, or his or her designee, shall be final" (§8.16.520(C)–(D)) Verified20

"Users desiring to dispute an administrative fine shall file a written request for the public works director to reconsider the fine along with full payment of the fine amount within thirty days" (§13.08.930(D)) Verified15

And for appeals: "Such decisions may be appealed pursuant to Section 23.01.692 and Government Code Section 53069.4" (§8.16.590(B)). The copy of the code we checked has no Section 23.01.692 Verified21,1.

The constitutional rules

  • The core of due process: "the opportunity to be heard 'at a meaningful time and in a meaningful manner'" Verified22.
  • How courts weigh a procedure (Mathews v. Eldridge): (1) "the private interest that will be affected," (2) "the risk of an erroneous deprivation of such interest through the procedures used, and the probable value, if any, of additional or substitute procedural safeguards," and (3) "the Government's interest" Verified22.
  • State law adds a court appeal. Within 20 days after the city's final decision, you can appeal to superior court, where the case "shall be heard de novo" (heard fresh) Verified23.

Our reading

Applying the Mathews factors: the private interest is money, sometimes a lot of it. The risk of error matters most when the deposit waiver depends on one employee whose decision "shall be final," with no stated standard for "actual financial inability." In the city's favor, a deposit discourages stalling, the waiver exists, the deposit is refunded with interest if you win (§8.16.540(D)) Verified20, and a de novo court appeal follows. The sewer rule (§13.08.930(D)) requires "full payment" and mentions no waiver at all. A court would weigh all of this; we can't predict the result. The broken cross-reference in §8.16.590(B) is a fair-notice problem the council could fix in one line.

What helps you: if you can't pay, apply for the waiver in writing, attach proof, and keep a copy. Raise the due-process point in writing at every step.

4. Vague nuisance words vs. fair warning

The Williams text

The nuisance list in §8.16.040 includes "Making or emitting any noise uncomfortable to or annoying to a reasonable person," "any condition or use which poses a threat to the public health or safety," and buildings that become "a harbor for vagrants, criminals or immoral persons." Separately, any code violation "shall be deemed to be a public nuisance" (§1.12.050) Verified24,14.

The constitutional rules

  • Fair warning and limits on enforcers: "Vague laws may trap the innocent by not providing fair warning," and "laws must provide explicit standards for those who apply them" (Grayned, 1972) Verified25.
  • "Annoying" alone failed. Cincinnati's ban on conduct "annoying to persons passing by" was struck down as "unconstitutionally vague," because "Conduct that annoys some people does not annoy others" (Coates, 1971) Verified26.

Our reading

Williams' noise clause is not the Cincinnati ordinance. It adds "a reasonable person," an objective standard courts often use to cure this kind of vagueness. Grayned itself held that Rockford's antinoise ordinance "is not invalid on its face" Verified25. "Immoral persons" has no such anchor, and it describes people, not a condition of property. That makes it the phrase most open to the warnings in Grayned. A vagueness challenge is usually raised by someone actually cited under the words in question.

What would help everyone: measurable standards (decibel limits, quiet hours, specific code sections) instead of adjectives.

5. Charging discretion vs. equal protection

The Williams text

"every act prohibited, declared unlawful or declared substandard or unsafe and every failure to perform an act made mandatory by this Code or any other ordinance of the City of Williams shall constitute either a misdemeanor or an infraction, at the discretion of the city attorney." (§1.12.010) Verified14

Why the choice matters

A misdemeanor is "punishable by a fine not exceeding one thousand dollars or by imprisonment not exceeding six months, or by both" (§1.12.020) Verified14. An infraction can't bring jail, but a person charged with one gets no jury and, in most cases, no appointed lawyer Verified27. State law makes an ordinance violation "a misdemeanor unless by ordinance it is made an infraction" Verified28.

The constitutional rules

  • Fair on paper isn't enough. A law "applied and administered by public authority with an evil eye and an unequal hand" violates equal protection (Yick Wo) Verified29.
  • A "class of one" can sue, alleging intentional different treatment from "others similarly situated" with "no rational basis for the difference" (Olech) Verified30.
  • But discretion itself is allowed. "The allegedly 'unequal' treatment which may result from simple laxity of enforcement or the nonarbitrary selective enforcement of a statute has never been considered a denial of equal protection." What's forbidden is "deliberate invidious discrimination," which a defendant raises by a motion to dismiss (Murgia) Verified31.
  • California adds its own words: no "privileges or immunities not granted on the same terms to all citizens" (Art. I, §7(b)) Verified3.

Our reading

Giving a prosecutor discretion is normal and isn't unconstitutional in itself. What equal protection catches is how discretion is used: who got a misdemeanor and who got a ticket for the same thing, and why. That can only be shown with records. The code lists no factors for the choice, and we have found no published policy. Records that would show it: counts of misdemeanor and infraction filings by code section, and any written charging policy (how to ask).

6. Fees set by resolution vs. Proposition 218

The Williams text

"as set forth in a schedule of fines established by resolution of the city council." (§8.16.490(A)) Verified20

"Any other fees the city may deem necessary to carry out this chapter, established from time to time by the city council." (§13.08.040(E)) Verified7

The constitutional rules

  • Fines are a separate category. A "fine, penalty, or other monetary charge imposed ... as a result of a violation of law" is excluded from "tax" (Art. XIII C, §1(e)(5)). Setting a fine schedule by resolution doesn't trigger Prop 218's vote rules Verified3.
  • Property-related fees are covered. The Constitution itself names "sewer, water, and refuse collection services" (§6(c)), and the California Supreme Court treats ongoing water delivery charges as property-related fees, though one-time charges for a new service connection are not (Bighorn, describing an earlier case) Verified3,32. Before a new or higher property-related fee: mailed notice, a hearing at least 45 days later, and no fee if "a majority of owners of the identified parcels" protest in writing (Art. XIII D, §6(a)). Sewer, water and trash are exempt from the separate vote requirement, but not from the protest hearing (§6(c)) Verified3.
  • Every property-related fee is capped by cost and can't fund unrelated things, and in court "the burden shall be on the agency to demonstrate compliance" (§6(b)) Verified3.
  • Other fees: for a charge that isn't a property-related fee, the city must show it "is not a tax" and is "no more than necessary to cover the reasonable costs" (XIII C, §1(e)) Verified3.

Our reading

"Any other fees ... established from time to time" is an open door in the text Verified7. Whether a given sewer fee went through Prop 218's notice and protest steps depends on what the city actually did when it adopted that fee. We haven't checked Williams' rate history and don't claim it fell short. Records that would show it: the mailed Prop 218 notice for each water and sewer rate change, the protest count from the hearing, and the rate study showing cost. Voters can also reduce or repeal a water or sewer charge by initiative (XIII C, §3; Bighorn) Verified3,32.

7. Liens and tax sales vs. takings and due process

The Williams text

"The property may be sold after three years by the tax collector for unpaid delinquent assessments. The tax collector's power of sale shall not be affected by the failure of the property owner to receive notice." (§8.16.180(C)) Verified33

"The failure of the clerk to record such lien claim or to mail such notice, or the failure of the owner to receive such notice, shall not affect the right to foreclose the lien for unpaid water bills" (§13.04.221(E)–(F)) Verified6

The constitutional rules

  • No keeping the surplus. A government that sells property over a debt "could not use the tax debt to confiscate more property than was due"; "a taxpayer is entitled to the surplus in excess of the debt owed" (Tyler v. Hennepin County, 2023) Verified34.
  • Notice is part of due process under both constitutions Verified5,3: the chance to be heard "at a meaningful time and in a meaningful manner" Verified22.
  • The tax-sale wording follows state law almost word for word Verified35.

Our reading

Because §8.16.180(C) tracks a state statute, a challenge would be to the state scheme, not just Williams' version. Tyler doesn't stop a sale; it protects the owner's equity beyond the debt. The "failure ... to receive notice" language in §13.04.221 raises a notice question, since the water-lien foreclosure goes through the city attorney rather than the county tax collector. We have not confirmed the state-law basis for that foreclosure or whether the city has used it.

8. Open government: the Brown Act, the CPRA and the Political Reform Act

The constitutional base

The right to see "the meetings of public bodies and the writings of public officials" is in the California Constitution itself, and "each local agency" must comply with the Public Records Act and the Brown Act (Art. I, §3(b)(1), (7)) Verified3. Speaking at a council meeting is petitioning, which the Supreme Court has called "one of the most precious of the liberties safeguarded by the Bill of Rights" (Lozman) Verified36.

The statutes that carry it out

  • Brown Act. The council must let the public speak on matters within its jurisdiction and may not prohibit "public criticism" (Gov. Code §54954.3) Verified37. Before suing over a violation, send a cure-and-correct demand Verified38. Brown Act guide.
  • Public Records Act. The agency must respond to a request within 10 days, extendable in unusual circumstances Verified39. CPRA guide.
  • Political Reform Act. Its findings say "State and local government should serve the needs and respond to the wishes of all citizens equally, without regard to their wealth," and "Public officials, whether elected or appointed, should perform their duties in an impartial manner, free from bias caused by their own financial interests or the financial interests of persons who have supported them" (Gov. Code §81001(a)–(b)) Verified40. The city clerk is the filing officer for Form 700 statements of economic interests Verified41, which are public records.

Our reading

These three laws are how you check everything else on this page. Discretion is only as fair as its record. If you want to know whether the city enforces evenly, charges fair fees or follows its own procedures, the answer is in documents you have a constitutional right to see.

9. New city taxes vs. Articles XIII A and XIII C

The constitutional rules

  • General taxes need a majority vote of the people. "No local government may impose, extend, or increase any general tax unless and until that tax is submitted to the electorate and approved by a majority vote" (Art. XIII C, §2(b)) Verified3.
  • Special taxes need two-thirds. "No local government may impose, extend, or increase any special tax unless and until that tax is submitted to the electorate and approved by a two-thirds vote" (Art. XIII C, §2(d)) Verified3.
  • Timing. A general tax election "shall be consolidated with a regularly scheduled general election for members of the governing body of the local government, except in cases of emergency declared by a unanimous vote of the governing body" (§2(b)) Verified3.
  • General law cities' taxing power comes with voter approval: Gov. Code §37100.5 lets them levy charter-city taxes "subject to the voters' approval pursuant to Article XIIIA" Verified42.

Our reading

The council can't raise or add a Williams tax by itself; it can only put one on the ballot. The disputes come at the edges: whether a charge is a "tax" or a fee (see fees and the XIII C lesson). Records that would show it: the resolution placing a measure on the ballot, the ballot text, and the certified results.

10. The ballot as a check on the council

The constitutional rules

  • Local initiative and referendum are constitutional powers "under procedures that the Legislature shall provide" (Art. II, §11(a)) Verified3.
  • Recall of city officers comes from Art. II, §19 and the Elections Code recall chapter, which covers "all counties, cities" (Elec. Code §11000) Verified3,43. Signature counts for Williams depend on its registered voters (Elec. Code §11221) Verified44.
  • Fees too. Voters can reduce or repeal a local tax, assessment, fee or charge by initiative (Art. XIII C, §3) Verified3.

Our reading

Every right on this page can be argued in court, but the ballot is usually faster and cheaper. If a code section worries enough residents, an initiative can change it; if the council passes an ordinance residents oppose, a referendum petition can hold it before it takes effect. Details and deadlines: Article II.

11. Where the city's power comes from, and where it stops

The rules

  • Williams "may make and enforce within its limits all local, police, sanitary, and other ordinances and regulations not in conflict with general laws" (Art. XI, §7) Verified3.
  • A conflicting local law "is preempted by such law and is void" (Sherwin-Williams) Verified45.
  • Williams is a general law city Verified46, so it has no charter to fall back on.

Our reading

Before asking whether a Williams section violates a constitution, ask whether it conflicts with a state statute. Preemption is often the simpler argument, and courts decide statutory questions before constitutional ones when they can. Method: A worked path.

What to do with this

  1. Learn the rule before you need it. Work through the U.S. and California lessons for each topic in the quick map. Bookmark this page and Who decides?.
  2. Ask for records, not opinions. Charging counts, fee notices, protest tallies, waiver decisions (CPRA guide).
  3. Ask the council in public. Suggested questions are on Who decides?.
  4. If it's your case, talk to a licensed California attorney. This site is education, not legal advice.

Check yourself

Answer in your head first, then open each question.

Does this site say any Williams code section is unconstitutional?

No. Only a court can decide that, usually on the facts of a real case.

Can the Williams council adopt a new general tax by itself?

No. It needs a majority vote of the electorate (Art. XIII C, §2(b)) Verified3.

What's often a simpler argument than a constitutional one against a city ordinance?

That it conflicts with a state statute and is preempted (Sherwin-Williams) Verified45.

What do courts weigh to decide whether a fine is excessive under California law?

Culpability, the relationship between harm and penalty, penalties in similar statutes, and ability to pay (R.J. Reynolds) Verified18.
