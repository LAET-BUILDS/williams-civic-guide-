The cases behind the rights, and where to read the originals

Lesson 68 of 68 · Cases and sources · 39 min read

A constitution's words are short. Courts decide what they mean in real disputes, and those decisions bind later cases. Below is every case this section relies on. For each one we read the opinion text ourselves; the link goes to a free copy. A case's holding is what it decided. Anything else in an opinion is explanation.

Read the whole opinion before you rely on a case. A quote out of context can mislead, and later cases can narrow or change a rule. We have not checked every later case that cites these decisions. Links go to free copies (Cornell's Legal Information Institute, CourtListener, Justia, and the California Attorney General). These are not the official reporters.

How to read a case citation

Citation Means
Camara v. Municipal Court, 387 U.S. 523 (1967) Volume 387 of the United States Reports, starting at page 523, decided in 1967. U.S. Supreme Court.
Amador Valley ... v. State Bd. of Equalization (1978) 22 Cal.3d 208 Volume 22 of California Reports, Third Series, page 208. California Supreme Court.
Gleaves v. Waters (1985) 175 Cal.App.3d 413 Volume 175 of California Appellate Reports, Third Series, page 413. A California Court of Appeal.

Which ones bind Williams? The California Supreme Court stated the rule in 1962: "Under the doctrine of stare decisis, all tribunals exercising inferior jurisdiction are required to follow decisions of courts exercising superior jurisdiction." "The decisions of this court are binding upon and must be followed by all the state courts of California," and Court of Appeal decisions (then called the District Courts of Appeal) "are binding upon all the justice and municipal courts and upon all the superior courts of this state" (Auto Equity Sales, Inc. v. Superior Court (1962) 57 Cal.2d 450) Verified1. That includes the Colusa County Superior Court, the trial court for the county Williams is in Unverified2. When appellate decisions conflict, "the court exercising inferior jurisdiction can and must make a choice between the conflicting decisions" Verified1. Only published Court of Appeal opinions count: an opinion "that is not certified for publication or ordered published must not be cited or relied on by a court or a party in any other action," with narrow exceptions (Cal. Rules of Court, rule 8.1115(a)) Verified3. On the U.S. Constitution, the U.S. Supreme Court has the last word for state courts and officials too: "the federal judiciary is supreme in the exposition of the law of the Constitution" (Cooper v. Aaron, 358 U.S. 1 (1958)) Verified4.

Holding vs. explanation. A case's holding is what it decided on its facts. The rest of the opinion explains. Later cases can narrow a holding, so read the newest cases first.

Key cases, explained

The tables below explain the cases the Williams lessons rely on most. The full case index lists every case in the course.

U.S. Supreme Court

Case What happened What the Court said Why it matters here
Marbury v. Madison (1803) A dispute over an undelivered judicial commission. "It is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is." Verified5 Courts, not cities, decide what the Constitution means.
Barron v. Baltimore (1833) A wharf owner said city work ruined his property. The takings clause "is intended solely as a limitation on the exercise of power by the government of the United States, and is not applicable to the legislation of the states." Verified6 Before the Fourteenth Amendment, the Bill of Rights didn't bind states or cities.
Yick Wo v. Hopkins (1886) San Francisco denied laundry permits to Chinese applicants under a neutral-sounding ordinance. A law "fair on its face" but applied "with an evil eye and an unequal hand" denies equal protection; the Fourteenth Amendment "is not confined to the protection of citizens." Verified7 Equal protection covers how a city enforces, and covers non-citizens.
Camara v. Municipal Court (1967) A San Francisco tenant was charged with a crime for refusing a warrantless housing inspection. He "had a constitutional right to insist that the inspectors obtain a warrant to search." Verified8 Code inspectors need consent or a warrant, except in emergencies.
Coates v. City of Cincinnati (1971) A city made it a crime to be "annoying to persons passing by." "Unconstitutionally vague"; "Conduct that annoys some people does not annoy others." Verified9 Vague city rules can fail.
Grayned v. City of Rockford (1972) A protester challenged two city ordinances. Explained why vague laws fail; upheld the antinoise ordinance as "not invalid on its face." Verified10 The test for vagueness, and a reminder that clear noise rules can stand.
Mathews v. Eldridge (1976) Disability benefits were cut off before a hearing. Due process means being heard "at a meaningful time and in a meaningful manner," judged by three factors. Verified11 The yardstick for any city procedure, including deposits to contest.
PruneYard Shopping Center v. Robins (1980) California protected petitioning at a private mall. States have a "sovereign right to adopt in its own constitution individual liberties more expansive than those conferred by the Federal Constitution." Verified12 California's rights can go further than federal rights.
United States v. Bajakajian (1998) The government sought to take $357,144 for a reporting violation. A punitive forfeiture is unconstitutional "if it is grossly disproportional to the gravity of the offense." Verified13 The federal test for excessive fines.
Village of Willowbrook v. Olech (2000) A village demanded a bigger easement from one family than from others to connect water. A "class of one" can claim equal protection. Verified14 One resident treated differently can sue, if there's no rational basis.
McDonald v. Chicago (2010) A challenge to a city handgun ban. "The Fourteenth Amendment incorporates the Second Amendment right." (from the official syllabus) Verified15 A modern example of incorporation against a city.
Lozman v. City of Riviera Beach (2018) A critic was handcuffed and removed during council public comment. "The existence of probable cause does not bar Lozman's First Amendment retaliation claim under the circumstances of this case." Verified16 Retaliating against a speaker at a council meeting can be challenged.
Timbs v. Indiana (2019) Indiana took a $42,000 vehicle over a drug sale. "The Excessive Fines Clause is therefore incorporated by the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment." Verified17 Excessive-fines protection binds states and cities.
Knick v. Township of Scott (2019) A township ordinance required public access to a private cemetery. A property owner "may bring a Fifth Amendment claim under §1983" when property is taken without compensation. Verified18 Takings claims against local government can go straight to federal court.
Tyler v. Hennepin County (2023) A county sold a condo over a tax debt of about $15,000 and kept the $25,000 surplus. "A taxpayer is entitled to the surplus in excess of the debt owed." Verified19 Government can't keep more than it's owed in a tax sale.

California courts

Case What happened What the court said Why it matters here
Murgia v. Municipal Court (1975) 15 Cal.3d 286 Farm-worker union members said they were prosecuted because of their union. A prosecution based on invidious discrimination "must collapse upon the sands of prejudice," but simple laxity or "nonarbitrary selective enforcement" is not a violation. Verified20 How to raise discriminatory enforcement, and how hard it is.
Hale v. Morgan (1978) 22 Cal.3d 388 A $100-a-day civil penalty reached $17,300. A "mandatory, fixed, substantial and cumulative punitive sanction" applied to people of very different culpability is "manifestly suspect." Verified21 Daily penalties that stack can be challenged.
Robins v. Pruneyard (1979) 23 Cal.3d 899 Students gathered petition signatures at a private mall. Art. I, §§2 and 3 "protect speech and petitioning, reasonably exercised, in shopping centers even when the centers are privately owned." Verified22 California speech rights reach further than federal ones.
Gleaves v. Waters (1985) 175 Cal.App.3d 413 A dispute over warrantless entry to abate a nuisance. Officials abating a nuisance "must have a warrant to enter any private property where such entry would invade a constitutionally protected privacy interest," absent consent or emergency. Verified23 The warrant rule applies to nuisance abatement in California.
Sherwin-Williams Co. v. City of Los Angeles (1993) 4 Cal.4th 893 Paint companies said state law preempted a Los Angeles rule on the retail display of aerosol paint. The court held it was not preempted. Local law that "duplicates, contradicts, or enters an area fully occupied by general law" is preempted and "void." Verified24 The test for when state law beats a city ordinance, and a reminder that cities often win.
Hill v. NCAA (1994) 7 Cal.4th 1 College athletes challenged drug testing by a private association. The privacy right can be enforced against private entities, and the claim starts with "a specific, legally protected privacy interest." Verified25 California's privacy right is broad.
People ex rel. Lockyer v. R.J. Reynolds Tobacco Co. (2005) 37 Cal.4th 707 Penalties for handing out free cigarette samples. Four factors for excessive fines, including "the defendant's ability to pay." Verified26 The California test for excessive fines.
Bighorn-Desert View Water Agency v. Verjil (2006) 39 Cal.4th 205 A resident's initiative to cut water rates and require votes on increases. Voters can reduce water charges by initiative, but can't impose a vote requirement on future increases this way. Verified27 What Prop 218 lets residents do about water rates.
