# The three layers of law

Every rule in Williams sits under two other layers of law. When you read a city rule, you are also asking: does it fit the layers above it?

{{layers-diagram}}

## Layer 1: Federal

- The U.S. Constitution says that it, and federal laws made under it, are "the supreme Law of the Land," and that state judges are bound by them, "any Thing in the Constitution or Laws of any State to the Contrary notwithstanding" (Article VI) [[V:archives-constitution]]. This is the [Supremacy Clause](/glossary/#supremacy-clause). [Read Article VI](/documents/us-constitution/#article-vi).
- The Fourteenth Amendment says "No State shall make or enforce any law" that abridges the privileges or immunities of citizens, and no state shall deprive any person of life, liberty, or property "without due process of law," or deny anyone "the equal protection of the laws" [[V:archives-11-27]]. [Read the Fourteenth Amendment](/documents/us-constitution/#amendment-xiv).
- Courts have held that most of the Bill of Rights (such as free speech and protection from unreasonable searches) also limits state and local governments through the Fourteenth Amendment, and that a city is bound the same way the state is [[U:research-notes]].

## Layer 2: State

- The California Constitution has its own list of rights in Article I, for example free speech (§2), the right to assemble and petition and to see public meetings and records (§3), due process and equal protection (§7), and protection from unreasonable searches (§13) [[V:senate-constitutions]]. [Read Article I](/documents/ca-constitution/article-i/).
- Article XI, §7 sets the basic rule for cities: "A county or city may make and enforce within its limits all local, police, sanitary, and other ordinances and regulations not in conflict with general laws" [[V:senate-constitutions]]. [Read Article XI, §7](/documents/ca-constitution/article-xi/#article-xi-sec-7).
- State statutes are organized into codes, such as the Government Code and the Elections Code. The official place to read them is leginfo.legislature.ca.gov [[U:leginfo-home]].

## Layer 3: City

- Williams is a [general law city](/glossary/#general-law-city) [[V:city-community]]. In plain terms, its power to make rules comes from state law, and its ordinances may not conflict with the state's [general laws](/glossary/#general-laws) [[U:research-notes]].
- The Williams Municipal Code (WMC) is the city's collection of [ordinances](/glossary/#ordinance) passed by the City Council [[U:research-notes]]. The official version is on Municode [[V:wmc-municode]]; a third-party copy on GoCodebook is easier to search but is not official [[V:wmc-gocodebook]].
- The layers point to each other. For example, the code's City Council chapter (ch. 2.04) refers readers to state law: "For statutory provisions on city council meetings, see Gov. Code §§ 36801—36815 and §§ 54950—54961" (third-party copy) [[V:wmc-gocodebook]].

<h2 id="preemption">Preemption: when a city rule loses</h2>

[Preemption](/glossary/#preemption) means a higher law pushes aside a lower one. California courts have described three ways a local rule can conflict with state law [[U:research-notes]]:

1. **It duplicates state law** (it repeats what the state already covers).
2. **It contradicts state law** (it requires what state law bans, or bans what state law requires).
3. **It enters a field the state fully occupies** (the state has covered the subject so completely that there is no room for local rules). This is called [field preemption](/glossary/#field-preemption).

Whether a specific Williams rule is preempted is a question for a court, not for this site. [The Method](/method/) helps you gather the facts and questions you would need.

## Which rule wins? Check yourself

Open each question to see the answer. These are general patterns, not answers about any real Williams rule.

<details class="quiz"><summary>1. A state law conflicts with the U.S. Constitution. Which wins?</summary>
<p><strong>The U.S. Constitution.</strong> Article VI makes it "the supreme Law of the Land," binding state judges even if a state's own constitution or laws say otherwise [[V:archives-constitution]].</p>
</details>

<details class="quiz"><summary>2. A Williams ordinance clearly conflicts with a general state law. Which wins?</summary>
<p><strong>Usually the state law.</strong> Article XI, §7 lets a city make ordinances only if they are "not in conflict with general laws" [[V:senate-constitutions]]. Courts treat a conflicting local rule as void [[U:research-notes]]. Whether there really is a conflict is often the hard part.</p>
</details>

<details class="quiz"><summary>3. State law says nothing about a subject. Can Williams make a rule about it?</summary>
<p><strong>Often yes, but check.</strong> Article XI, §7 gives cities general power to make local rules [[V:senate-constitutions]]. You would still check whether the state has occupied the field, and whether the rule respects constitutional rights such as free speech and due process [[U:research-notes]].</p>
</details>

<details class="quiz"><summary>4. A city rule is stricter than a state law on the same subject. Is it allowed?</summary>
<p><strong>It depends.</strong> A court would ask whether the city rule duplicates or contradicts state law, or enters a field the state fully occupies, and whether the state law itself says what cities may do [[U:research-notes]]. Read the state statute closely (Method step 3).</p>
</details>
