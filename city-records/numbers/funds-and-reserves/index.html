Lesson 2: Funds, reserves and deficits

On this page (3 sections)
  1. The numbers
  2. What to ask
  3. Sources cited on this page

DRAFT. This lesson is not final.

The idea: incentives, and who decides. In Basic Economics, Sowell teaches readers to judge a policy by the incentives it creates, not only by the goals it announces Unverified1. For a budget, that means asking: who decides where the money goes, who bears the cost, and what does the way the accounts are set up encourage?

Key takeaways

  • A city keeps money in separate funds. A fund that runs short can be covered by cash from another fund.
  • The General Fund balance fell from $8.8 million to $6.0 million in FY2025 Verified2.
  • At the end of August 2026, about $2.0 million of General Fund cash was set against deficits in other funds Verified3Calculated3.

The numbers

1. The General Fund balance, FY2025. The audit says the balance went from $8,816,153 to $6,035,850, a drop of $2,780,303. Subtract the Streets fund deficit of $1,261,520 and the remaining amount is $4,774,330. The audit says these "net" reserves fell 33.4% (by $2,390,369) from $7,164,699, and that staff link the drop to the General Fund's $2,817,856 share of paying off a pension debt Verified2.

2. Cash on August 31, 2026. The city's treasurer's report shows how General Fund cash is set against deficits in other funds Verified3:

Item, Aug 31, 2026 Amount
General Fund cash and investments $6,927,287
Less Storm Drain fund deficit ($43,162)
Less Streets fund cash deficit ($1,659,902)
Less Morning Star street loan ($322,464)
Net General Fund balance $4,901,759

The three deficits add up to $2,025,528 Calculated3. The report also shows $390,099 left of a $2,000,000 General Fund pledge for the E Street project Verified3.

3. The water fund. The FY2026 budget shows the water fund taking in $1,225,441 and spending $1,318,378, a shortfall of $92,937 Verified4Calculated4. In the October 21, 2026 packet, a staff report on an E Street water and sewer project says the General Fund "will provide temporary cash shortfalls of up to $800,000" for the water fund, expected to be paid back by the end of FY2028 Verified3.

What to ask

None of this says anything is wrong. Covering one fund with another's cash is a way to manage timing. But it shows why the questions matter:

  • Who decides? The municipal code says the Council adopts the budget and holds public hearings on it Verified5. See Who decides? for who holds which choices.
  • Who bears the cost if a fund can't pay it back? Water and sewer are paid by utility bills, not the General Fund Verified4.
  • What does the setup encourage? If a shortfall in one fund is quietly covered by another, how would a resident see it? Where is it in the packet?
