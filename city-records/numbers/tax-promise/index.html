Lesson 4: What a tax promise really costs

On this page (5 sections)
  1. What the measure said
  2. The math: what is it per person?
  3. Who pays? (U)
  4. "And then what?" and results
  5. Sources cited on this page

DRAFT. This lesson is not final. It takes no position on Measure F.

The ideas: who pays, "and then what?", and results over goals. Sowell's books ask readers to look past the stated goal. Applied Economics looks at later effects Unverified1. Basic Economics teaches judging a policy by the incentives it creates rather than the goals it announces Unverified2. A tax promise is a goal. This lesson practices asking what it costs, who pays, and how you would know it worked.

Key takeaways

  • Measure F was a one-half percent city sales tax for fire protection, estimated at about $1.2 million a year Verified3.
  • Divided across all residents, $1.2 million is about $215 per person per year (C) Calculated3,4. That is an average, not a bill.
  • Part of a sales tax can be paid by people who do not live here (U) Unverified3.

What the measure said

The city's notice put Measure F on the Nov 3, 2026 ballot as a "Special Transactions and Use (Sales) Tax." The ballot question asks about enacting a one-half percent city special sales tax, "generating approximately $1.2 million annually," for fire protection and emergency response through the Williams Fire Protection Authority. It says the tax would run "until ended by voters" and be "subject to annual audits and reporting and citizens' oversight committee." It needs approval by a majority of the Williams voters who vote on it Verified3.

The vote was held on November 3, 2026. We do not state a result here. See Colusa County Elections Verified5 for results once they are posted.

The math: what is it per person?

$1,200,000 ÷ 5,585 residents ≈ $215 per person per year (C). The city's estimate is "approximately" $1.2 million Verified3, and the Census counts 5,585 people in Williams (margin of error ±24) Verified4. The division is ours Calculated3,4.

Read this number carefully. It spreads the whole amount over every resident, children included. No one pays exactly $215. What a household pays depends on what it buys in Williams. The result also rests on the city's rough estimate.

Who pays? (U)

A sales tax is paid when people buy things. Williams sells to people who live elsewhere, too. The city's audit says a large part of Williams sales tax comes from gasoline sales Verified6. Our inference (U): some of the $1.2 million would likely be paid by people who do not live in Williams, such as travelers, so residents' share could be lower than $215 per person. We do not know how much Unverified3. This is not something the city's notice says.

"And then what?" and results

The notice describes how results would be checked: annual audits, reporting, and a citizens' oversight committee Verified3. In Sowell's terms, that is the difference between a goal and a result. A goal says what the money is for. A result is what you can count later. Questions you can ask of any tax promise:

  1. What is the number the promise is aiming at (for example, response times or staffing), and where will it be reported?
  2. If the tax ends or falls short, what then?
  3. How will you know it worked?
