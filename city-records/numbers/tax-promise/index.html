Lesson 4: What a tax promise really costs

On this page (5 sections)
  1. What the city promised
  2. The math: what is it per household?
  3. Who pays? (U)
  4. "And then what?" and results
  5. Sources cited on this page

DRAFT. This lesson is not final. It takes no position on whether any rate is right.

The ideas: who pays, "and then what?", and results over goals. Sowell's books ask readers to look past the stated goal. Applied Economics looks at later effects Unverified1. Basic Economics teaches judging a policy by the incentives it creates rather than the goals it announces Unverified2. A tax is a promise: you pay, and the government says what it will do with the money. So is a rate increase. This lesson uses the Williams water and sewer rate increase to practice asking what it costs, who pays, and how you would know it worked.

Key takeaways

  • The city says money from the water rate increase will be used to meet state and federal water quality laws and to "construct, repair, maintain and properly operate water supply infrastructure" Verified3.
  • For a typical home with a small meter, the fixed water and sewer charges go from $117.37 a month (Dec 2024) to $133.60 (Feb 2026) and $184.68 (Feb 2030). That is $67.31 a month more, about 57% (C) Calculated4.
  • The rates are charged per account, per month. Your own bill also depends on your meter size and how much water you use Verified4.

What the city promised

A rate increase is a trade. Customers pay more, and the city promises something in return. The city's utilities page says the city "is responsible for providing safe, high-quality, reliable water." It says the revenues "will be used to ensure the City can meet State and Federal water quality laws, and construct, repair, maintain and properly operate water supply infrastructure." On January 21, 2026, the City Council adopted a series of water rate adjustments for 2026 to 2030, and the first one took effect in February 2026 Verified3.

The city also posts water and sewer rate studies "detailing the needs for the water and sewer rate increases" Verified5. Those studies are where the city lays out its reasons. We have not used figures from them in this lesson.

The math: what is it per household?

For a home with a small (5/8 to 3/4 inch) meter, the city's schedule sets two fixed monthly charges: water and home sewer. Added together, they step up every February 1 Verified4:

Date Both fixed charges, per month
Dec 2024 $117.37
Feb 2026 $133.60
Feb 2027 $145.41
Feb 2028 $158.84
Feb 2029 $174.17
Feb 2030 $184.68

The totals are our addition of the city's two charges. From Dec 2024 to Feb 2030 that is $67.31 a month more, about 57%, or $807.72 more over a year at the 2030 level (C) Calculated4.

Read these numbers carefully. They are the fixed part only. The base charge includes up to 5 hundred cubic feet of water a month, and you pay a separate rate for each hundred cubic feet above that Verified4. Larger meters, apartments and businesses have different charges Verified4. Your real bill is probably different. Check it against the schedule.

Who pays? (U)

The city's rates are charged per account, per month Verified4. Our inference (U): the person on the account is billed. Whether that cost falls on an owner or on a tenant, for example through rent, depends on the home, and we have no data on it Unverified4. This is not something the city's schedule says.

"And then what?" and results

The schedule runs through February 2030, so the cost keeps stepping up for years Verified4. Lesson 5 covers how the vote happened. In Sowell's terms, the repairs and reliable service are the goal. A result is what you can count later. Questions you can ask of any rate increase:

  1. Which repairs or upgrades is the money for, and where will progress be reported?
  2. What does it cost one household each month at every step, and what happens if the work costs more than expected?
  3. How will you know the promised work got done?
