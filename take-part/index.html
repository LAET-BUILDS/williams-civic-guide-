This page shows how to follow the council, speak up and get records. It also covers the petition tools state law gives residents. This is unofficial education, not legal advice. Deadlines are strict. Check the statute, which is the written state law.

Read the agenda and packet

  • Official agenda page: cityofwilliams.org agendas and minutes. The agenda packet is a PDF. It has the agenda plus staff reports. A staff report is city staff's write-up on an item.

Public comment

  • General public comment comes near the start of the agenda. It was item D on the October 21, 2026 agenda. Use it for items not listed as public hearings. A public hearing is a set time for the public to speak on one item. To speak on an item that is on the agenda, wait until that item comes up.
  • The agenda asks speakers to use the podium. Give your name and say if you live in Williams. Name any group you speak for. The mayor may set a time limit based on how many people want to speak Verified3.
  • State law: the council must let the public speak on matters within its jurisdiction. Jurisdiction means the topics the council has power over. It may set reasonable time limits. But it may not prohibit criticism of the city's policies or actions. Speakers who use a translator get twice the time (Gov. Code §54954.3). Gov. Code is short for the California Government Code Verified6. Courts have upheld shorter time limits Unverified7.
  • The council generally cannot act on something that is not on the agenda. A comment can raise an issue. But it usually can't get a vote that night Verified4.
  • You can ask for an item on a future agenda. But that is a request, not a right. The council sets its meeting rules by resolution (WMC §2.04.020, third-party copy). A resolution is a formal council decision that is not a law. WMC means the Williams Municipal Code, the city's book of laws Verified8.

Tips: read the packet a few days ahead. Write down the item number. Bring a short written copy of your comment for the city clerk. That puts it in the record.

Brown Act: open meetings

The Brown Act is California's open-meetings law (Gov. Code §54950 and following). It says city meetings must be open to the public. The California Constitution also says "the meetings of public bodies and the writings of public officials and agencies shall be open to public scrutiny" (Art. I, §3(b)(1)) Verified9,10.

Cure-and-correct demand

Sometimes the council acts in a way that breaks certain open-meeting rules. Then you can ask it to undo the action (Gov. Code §54960.1). This is called a cure-and-correct demand. "Cure" means fix. Here are the steps:

  1. Send a written demand to the council. Describe the action and the violation.
  2. Deadline: 90 days after the action. The deadline is 30 days in one case: the action was not on a properly posted agenda for a meeting held in open session. Open session is the public part of a meeting.
  3. The council has 30 days to fix it or refuse. If it says nothing, that counts as a refusal.
  4. You then have 15 days to file in court.

There are exceptions. One is substantial compliance, which means the council mostly followed the rules. Others are some contracts that people relied on in good faith, and tax and debt actions Verified11.

More: see our Brown Act guide, section by section.

Public records

  • The California Public Records Act is the state law that lets you ask for city records. The city must tell you within 10 days if it has disclosable records. Disclosable means the law lets the city release them. In unusual cases, the city can add up to 14 days. The 10 days is to decide. It does not have to hand everything over by then Verified12.
  • If the city wrongly holds back records, you can go to court. If you win, the city can be made to pay your attorney's fees Verified13.
  • Where to send a request: the city clerk, 810 E Street, Williams, (530) 235-3274 Verified1. Describe the records as clearly as you can. Keep a copy. Write down the date you sent it.

More: see our Public Records Act guide.

Initiative and referendum

Referendum: challenge a new ordinance

  • A referendum lets voters challenge a new ordinance. The petition needs signatures from 10% of the city's voters. File it within 30 days of the date the city clerk attests the adopted ordinance. Attest means the clerk signs to certify it. The ordinance is then put on hold, and the council must reconsider it (Elec. Code §9237). Elec. Code is the California Elections Code. If the council does not fully repeal the ordinance, it must put it to a vote (§9241) Verified14,15.
  • It works on ordinances only, not resolutions. The council passes budgets, many fees and many contracts by resolution Unverified16.
  • A development agreement is a contract between the city and a builder. The city code says Williams development agreements fall under the 30-day referendum window (WMC §17.09, third-party copy) Verified8.

Initiative: propose a new ordinance

  • An initiative lets voters propose a new ordinance. The petition needs signatures from 10% of registered voters. The council must then do one of three things: adopt the measure, put it on the ballot, or order a report first (Elec. Code §9215) Verified17.
  • Williams had 2,276 registered voters in the Secretary of State's September 4, 2026 report. At that count, 10% means at least 228 signatures Calculated18,19. But the count that applies is the county's last voter report at the time the petition notice is published (§9215) Verified18,19. So confirm the number with Colusa County Elections Unverified20.
  • The California Constitution lets city voters use initiative and referendum "under procedures that the Legislature shall provide" (Art. II, §11) Verified10.
