Know your rights

Ten questions about your rights with city government. Open one for the full answer: the text of the law, the steps and deadlines, the court cases, what applies in Williams, what to do if the rules are not followed, and common misconceptions, with a source for each point.

Can the city raise my water rate?

What the Constitution says

Yes, but only by following Article XIII D, section 6 of the California Constitution, added by Proposition 218 in 1996. Water, sewer and trash charges are "property-related" fees, and the section says an agency "shall follow the procedures pursuant to this section in imposing or increasing any fee or charge" (Art. XIII D, §6(a)). The California Supreme Court treats ongoing water delivery charges as property-related fees; one-time charges for a new connection are not (Bighorn-Desert View Water Agency v. Verjil, 2006).

Before a new or higher rate, the city must identify every affected parcel, calculate the amount for each, and mail written notice "to the record owner of each identified parcel" giving "the amount of the fee or charge proposed," "the basis upon which the amount ... was calculated," "the reason for the fee or charge," and "the date, time, and location of a public hearing" (§6(a)(1)).

The hearing must be held "not less than 45 days after mailing the notice." At the hearing the city "shall consider all protests," and "if written protests against the proposed fee or charge are presented by a majority of owners of the identified parcels, the agency shall not impose the fee or charge" (§6(a)(2)).

Sources: Verified8,9

How a protest works, step by step

  • The notice. For an existing service, state law lets the city put the notice in your regular bill or mail it to the billing address; if the city wants to keep its power to put a lien on the parcel, it must also mail the record owner at the assessment-roll address when that is different (Gov. Code §53755(a)).
  • Who can protest. "One written protest per parcel, filed by an owner or tenant of the parcel, shall be counted" (§53755(b)). Renters can file the protest for their parcel, but each parcel counts once.
  • What counts. Only written protests count toward the majority (Art. XIII D, §6(a)(2)). Speaking at the hearing is allowed (Gov. Code §54954.3), but a spoken objection is not a written protest.
  • The count. The majority is measured against the number of identified parcels, not the number of people who show up (§6(a)(2)). The city must keep every written protest for at least two years after the hearing (§53755(d)), so the protests and the count can be requested under the Public Records Act during that time.
  • The deadline. Protests must be "presented" at the hearing (§6(a)(2)). Ask the City Clerk, 810 E Street, (530) 235-3274, how and by when the city accepts them for a given hearing.

Sources: Verified10,8,11,12,6

The limits on any water rate, even without a protest

Winning or losing a protest doesn't end the rules. Every existing, new or higher property-related fee must meet five tests (Art. XIII D, §6(b)):

  • Revenue "shall not exceed the funds required to provide the property-related service" (§6(b)(1)).
  • Revenue "shall not be used for any purpose other than that for which the fee or charge was imposed" (§6(b)(2)).
  • The charge on a parcel "shall not exceed the proportional cost of the service attributable to the parcel" (§6(b)(3)).
  • No charge for a service unless it is "actually used by, or immediately available to, the owner"; charges "based on potential or future use of a service are not permitted" (§6(b)(4)).
  • No property-related fee for "general governmental services" such as police, fire, ambulance or library services that are available to the public at large (§6(b)(5)).

In a lawsuit, "the burden shall be on the agency to demonstrate compliance with this article" (§6(b)). Water, sewer and trash are exempt from the separate vote that other property-related fees need, but not from the notice, hearing and protest (§6(c)).

Sources: Verified8

Rate schedules over several years

A water, sewer or trash agency may adopt a schedule of rates for up to five years that includes automatic inflation adjustments or pass-through increases from a wholesale supplier, if the schedule was adopted through the Prop. 218 process, any inflation adjustment doesn't exceed the cost of service, and notice of each adjustment is given at least 30 days before it takes effect (Gov. Code §53756).

This is why one hearing can set increases that arrive year after year: the protest happens once, when the schedule is adopted.

If the city doesn't follow the rules

  • Object in writing before the hearing. Since 2025, if the city follows a set of steps (posting the written basis for the fee online, giving at least 45 days to object, and answering each objection in writing before the hearing), a lawsuit claiming the water or sewer fee violates Article XIII D is barred unless the person "timely submitted to the local agency a written objection ... that specifies the grounds" (Gov. Code §53759.1(b)-(c)). The notice must say so in a "prominently displayed statement" (§53759.1(c)(6)). Read the notice for that statement.
  • Sue within 120 days. A court challenge to a water or sewer rate adopted or changed after Jan 1, 2022 must be filed within 120 days of the effective date or final adoption, whichever is later, and the mailed notice must state that limit (Gov. Code §53759(a), (d), (f)). Billing errors are not covered by that limit (§53759(e)).
  • Reduce the rate by initiative. The Constitution says the initiative power "shall not be prohibited or otherwise limited in matters of reducing or repealing any local tax, assessment, fee or charge" (Art. XIII C, §3). The Supreme Court held this "expressly authorizes initiative measures ... insofar as they seek to reduce or repeal a public agency's water rates," but not measures requiring a vote on future increases (Bighorn). See Initiative and referendum.

Sources: Verified21,22,8,9

Common misconceptions

  • "The council can raise water rates by a simple vote." Not without the mailed notice, the 45-day wait and the protest hearing (Art. XIII D, §6(a)).
  • "Water rate increases go on the ballot." Not automatically. Water, sewer and trash are exempt from the property-owner or voter election (§6(c)); residents can still propose a reduction by initiative (Art. XIII C, §3).
  • "Only owners can protest." The Constitution's notice goes to owners, but state law counts one protest per parcel from "an owner or tenant" (Gov. Code §53755(b)).
  • "Water money can fill other budget holes." Revenue can't be used for any other purpose (§6(b)(2)).

Sources: Verified8,9,10

Can I record a council meeting?

What the law says

Yes. "Any person attending an open and public meeting of a legislative body of a local agency shall have the right to record the proceedings in the absence of a reasonable finding by the legislative body of the local agency that the recording cannot continue without noise, illumination, or obstruction of view that constitutes, or would constitute, a persistent disruption of the proceedings" (Gov. Code §54953.5(a)).

Since Jan 1, 2026 the section says "record the proceedings" without listing particular devices, so it covers a phone as much as a camera. A separate section protects broadcasting: the council can't "prohibit or otherwise restrict the broadcast of its open and public meetings" without the same kind of finding about noise, light or blocked view (Gov. Code §54953.6). That covers livestreaming a meeting yourself.

The right comes from the California Constitution: "the meetings of public bodies and the writings of public officials and agencies shall be open to public scrutiny," and laws that further that access "shall be broadly construed" (Art. I, §3(b)(1)-(2)).

Sources: Verified23,24,25,8

How it works in the room

  • No permission needed. The right belongs to "any person attending"; there's no requirement to ask first or announce yourself (§54953.5(a)).
  • No sign-in needed. You can't be required "to register his or her name, to provide other information, to complete a questionnaire" as a condition of attending. Any sign-in sheet must say it is voluntary (Gov. Code §54953.3).
  • The only limit is disruption. The council can stop a recording only by making "a reasonable finding" that the recording itself causes noise, light or a blocked view amounting to "a persistent disruption" (§54953.5(a)). Quiet recording from a seat or the back of the room doesn't meet that test on its face. A bright light or a tripod in the aisle could.
  • What can't be recorded. Closed sessions are not open meetings, so the public isn't in the room (Gov. Code §54962). See "Can the council meet in private?" below.
  • Disrupting the meeting is a separate matter. If a meeting is "willfully interrupted" so that order can't be restored by removing the people responsible, the council may clear the room and continue, but press not involved in the disturbance may stay (Gov. Code §54957.9). Willfully disturbing a lawful meeting is a misdemeanor (Penal Code §403). Recording isn't disturbing.

Sources: Verified23,26,27,28,29

The city's own recordings

Any recording the city makes of an open meeting "shall be subject to inspection pursuant to the California Public Records Act," but "may be erased or destroyed 30 days after the recording." Inspection of an audio or video recording "shall be provided without charge on equipment made available by the local agency" (§54953.5(b)).

Williams streams and archives its meetings on AV Capture All, and the agenda calls the livestream "a courtesy". The law lets a city erase its recordings after 30 days (§54953.5(b)), so a copy you make yourself is the only one you control. If a past video matters, download it or request a copy from the City Clerk, (530) 235-3274, soon after the meeting.

Sources: Verified23,24,30,19,31

If you're told to stop

  • At the meeting. Ask, calmly and on the record, whether the council is making the finding §54953.5(a) requires: that your recording causes noise, light or a blocked view that is a persistent disruption. Keep the exchange short; the video itself will show what happened.
  • Afterwards: cease and desist. To challenge a past violation, state law requires a letter first. Send it by postal mail or fax to the City Clerk within nine months, clearly describing the action and the violation. The council has 30 days to respond. If it doesn't give an "unconditional commitment" to stop, you have 60 days to sue (Gov. Code §54960.2(a)-(b)).
  • Going to court. "Any interested person" can sue "for the purpose of stopping or preventing violations or threatened violations" (Gov. Code §54960(a)). A court may award costs and "reasonable attorney fees" to a plaintiff who proves a violation (Gov. Code §54960.5).
  • What doesn't apply. The cure-and-correct demand (§54960.1) lets you void an action taken in violation of §§54953, 54954.2, 54954.5, 54954.6, 54956 or 54956.5. The recording section (§54953.5) isn't on that list, so being stopped from recording doesn't by itself void the council's votes that night.

Sources: Verified23,32,33,34,35

Common misconceptions

  • "You need the mayor's permission." The statute gives the right to the person attending; the only exception is a reasonable finding of persistent disruption (§54953.5(a)).
  • "The city's livestream means you don't need to record." The city may erase its recordings after 30 days (§54953.5(b)), and Williams calls its stream a courtesy.
  • "Speakers can refuse to be recorded." The right is to record "the proceedings," and public comment is part of them (§54953.5(a)). The statute has no opt-out for speakers.
  • "Cities can set stricter rules." A city can give more access than the Brown Act requires, not less (Gov. Code §54953.7).

Sources: Verified23,19,36

Can I speak about anything at a meeting?

What the law says

About anything within the council's "subject matter jurisdiction," which in practice means anything the city can act on. "Every agenda for regular meetings shall provide an opportunity for members of the public to directly address the legislative body on any item of interest to the public, before or during the legislative body's consideration of the item, that is within the subject matter jurisdiction of the legislative body" (Gov. Code §54954.3(a)(1)).

Two rules protect what you say. "The legislative body of a local agency shall not prohibit public criticism of the policies, procedures, programs, or services of the agency, or of the acts or omissions of the legislative body" (§54954.3(c)). And the California Constitution gives the people "the right to instruct their representatives, petition government for redress of grievances, and assemble freely to consult for the common good" (Art. I, §3(a)). The U.S. Supreme Court has called the right to petition "one of the most precious of the liberties safeguarded by the Bill of Rights" (Lozman).

The protection has an edge: §54954.3(c) doesn't "confer any privilege or protection for expression beyond that otherwise provided by law". Speech that is unprotected elsewhere, such as true threats, stays unprotected at the podium.

Sources: Verified11,37,8,38

How public comment works

  • Agenda items. You can speak on an item "before or during" the council's consideration of it (§54954.3(a)(1)).
  • Topics not on the agenda. You can raise them in general public comment, but "no action or discussion shall be undertaken on any item not appearing on the posted agenda." Council members or staff may "briefly respond," ask clarifying questions, refer the matter to staff, ask staff to report back, or "take action to direct staff to place a matter of business on a future agenda" (Gov. Code §54954.2(a)(3)). The last one is how a comment turns into a future vote.
  • Special meetings. You can speak only on items described in the meeting notice (§54954.3(a)(3)), because no other business may be considered at a special meeting (Gov. Code §54956(a)).
  • Time limits. The council may adopt "reasonable regulations," including limits on "the total amount of time allocated for public testimony on particular issues and for each individual speaker" (§54954.3(b)(1)).
  • Interpreters. If the council limits time, a speaker who uses a translator gets "at least twice the allotted time," unless the council uses simultaneous translation equipment (§54954.3(b)(2)-(3)).
  • Your name. Attendance can't be conditioned on giving your name (Gov. Code §54953.3).

Sources: Verified11,39,40,41,26

If you're cut off or silenced

  • Viewpoint matters. A time limit applied evenly is allowed (§54954.3(b)(1)); stopping a speaker because they criticize the city or the council is what §54954.3(c) forbids.
  • Cease and desist, then court. Send a letter to the City Clerk by mail or fax within nine months describing what happened; the council has 30 days to respond with an unconditional commitment, and you have 60 days after that to sue (Gov. Code §54960.2). Any interested person can sue to stop or prevent violations (§54960(a)), and a winning plaintiff may recover attorney fees (§54960.5).
  • What doesn't apply. §54954.3 isn't on the list of sections whose violation can void a council action through a cure-and-correct demand (§54960.1(a)). If the problem is that the council acted on something not on the posted agenda, that is §54954.2, which is on the list: send the written demand within 30 days of the action (§54960.1(c)(1)).

Sources: Verified11,32,33,34,35

Common misconceptions

  • "The council has to answer my question." It may briefly respond, but the law doesn't require an answer, and it bars discussion of items not on the agenda (§54954.2(a)(3)).
  • "I can only talk about agenda items." General public comment covers anything within the council's jurisdiction (§54954.3(a)(1)).
  • "Criticizing a council member by name is out of order." Criticism of "the acts or omissions of the legislative body" can't be prohibited (§54954.3(c)).
  • "My comment will get a vote tonight." Not if it isn't on the agenda; ask the council to direct staff to place it on a future agenda (§54954.2(a)(3)).

Sources: Verified39,11

Can a city inspector come into my home?

What the Constitution says

Not without your consent or an inspection warrant signed by a judge, except in an emergency. In Camara v. Municipal Court (1967), the U.S. Supreme Court held that housing-code inspections are searches under the Fourth Amendment: "except in certain carefully defined classes of cases, a search of private property without proper consent is 'unreasonable' unless it has been authorized by a valid search warrant." The tenant there "had a constitutional right to insist that the inspectors obtain a warrant to search" and "may not constitutionally be convicted for refusing to consent to the inspection".

The same day, See v. City of Seattle extended this to businesses: "administrative entry, without consent, upon the portions of commercial premises which are not open to the public may only be compelled through prosecution or physical force within the framework of a warrant procedure".

California courts apply it to nuisance abatement: "In the absence of consent or exigent circumstances, government officials engaged in the abatement of a public nuisance must have a warrant to enter any private property where such entry would invade a constitutionally protected privacy interest" (Gleaves v. Waters, 1985). The court limited this to entries that "infringe upon constitutionally recognized expectations of privacy".

Sources: Verified43,44,45

How an inspection warrant works

California's inspection-warrant law is Code of Civil Procedure §§1822.50 to 1822.59:

  • What it is. "An order, in writing, in the name of the people, signed by a judge of a court of record," for an inspection "relating to building, fire, safety, plumbing, electrical, health, labor, or zoning" (§1822.50).
  • The application. It must be supported by an affidavit "particularly describing" the place and the purpose, and must state "that consent to inspect has been sought and refused" or give facts justifying not asking (§1822.51). The judge may question the applicant under oath and must be satisfied the grounds exist (§1822.53).
  • The standard. Cause exists if "reasonable legislative or administrative standards for conducting a routine or area inspection are satisfied" for the property, or "there is reason to believe that a condition of nonconformity exists" there (§1822.52). This is lower than criminal probable cause: Camara said a warrant may rest on area-wide inspection standards, which "may be based upon the passage of time, the nature of the building ... or the condition of the entire area".
  • Limits on the warrant. It must describe each place to be inspected and "the purpose and limitations of the inspection" (§1822.54). It lasts no more than 14 days unless the judge extends it, and is void if not carried out in time (§1822.55).
  • How it is carried out. No inspection between 6:00 p.m. and 8:00 a.m., and not when no owner or occupant is present, unless the judge specifically allows it. No forcible entry unless the judge expressly authorizes it for a suspected immediate threat to health or safety or after earlier attempts to serve a warrant failed. Where you refused consent, you must get notice "at least 24 hours before the warrant is executed," unless the judge finds immediate execution necessary (§1822.56).
  • Refusing a warrant. Once there is a warrant, willfully refusing to permit the inspection is a misdemeanor (§1822.57). The right is to insist on a warrant, not to block one.

Sources: Verified46,47,48,49,43,50,51,52,53

Exceptions

  • Emergencies. The warrant rule doesn't "foreclose prompt inspections, even without a warrant, that the law has traditionally upheld in emergency situations" (Camara).
  • Consent. If you or another person with authority let the inspector in, there is no warrant question (Camara: a search "without proper consent" needs a warrant).
  • Areas without a privacy interest. Gleaves applies the warrant rule only where entry would invade a constitutionally protected privacy interest, and See covers only the parts of a business "not open to the public".

Sources: Verified43,45,44

In Williams: what the city code says

  • Nuisance abatement. If the owner doesn't give written consent, the city enters with verbal permission, an inspection warrant or another lawful way (Williams Municipal Code §8.16.120(F)).
  • Garbage. The refuse chapter allows entry "after obtaining either a warrant or permission to do so from the owner or occupant of the premises" (§8.08.090).
  • Water and sewer. The water chapter gives a "right of entering any or all premises for the purpose of determining whether the provisions of this chapter are being complied with" (§13.04.310), and the sewer chapter says staff "shall be permitted to enter all properties from which wastes or wastewaters are being or are capable of being discharged into the sewage system" (§13.08.060). Neither mentions a warrant or consent.
  • Refusing entry and water service. "If any water user refuses entry by, or hinders or prevents inspection by the director or his representative, then the city may discontinue water service to the premises until and unless entry is allowed" (§13.04.220(E)). Camara involved a criminal penalty for refusing; whether a utility shut-off is treated the same way is a question we know of no court answering for Williams. See the Williams entry lesson.
  • Code text. These quotes come from a third-party copy of the code (GoCodebook); the official code is on Municode. Check a quote there or with the City Clerk before relying on it.

Sources: Verified54,55,56,57,43,42,58

In Williams: what happens after an inspection

An inspection often leads to one of two papers, each with its own deadline:

  • Notice to abate. It orders you to fix a nuisance by a compliance date and is posted on the property and delivered in person or by certified mail (§8.16.110(B)). To contest it, file a written hearing request with the City Clerk within 15 days (§8.16.110(A)). If you don't, the nuisance is treated as confirmed (§8.16.110(E)). The hearing is within 45 days of your request, the city must prove the nuisance by a preponderance of the evidence, and the written decision is due within 10 days (§§8.16.110(D), 8.16.120(C), 8.16.130). If the city fixes the problem itself, the cost can become a lien or special assessment (§8.16.140).
  • Administrative citation. A fine with a correction date. Ask for a hearing within 21 days of the citation, with the fine as a deposit or an approved waiver; a waiver request goes to the finance officer within 10 days (§§8.16.510, 8.16.520). The hearing officer can't be someone who investigated or enforced the case (§8.16.475(C)), and you can question the officer, present evidence and bring a lawyer (§8.16.530). Not showing up forfeits the fine and your right to go to court (§8.16.570(A)).
  • Court. After a citation hearing, you can appeal to the Colusa County Superior Court within 20 days after the decision is served; the court hears the case fresh (Gov. Code §53069.4(b)(1)). The court has two locations, both in Colusa: the Main Courthouse, 547 Market St., and the Courthouse Annex, 532 Oak St. Call (530) 458-5149 to ask where to file.
  • Contacts. Code Enforcement, 700 North Street, (530) 473-2533. Building, 460 8th Street, (530) 235-3276. City Clerk, 810 E Street, (530) 235-3274.

Sources: Verified54,59,60,61,6

At the door

  • You can ask the inspector's name, department and purpose, and whether they have a warrant. A warrant must describe the place and "the purpose and limitations of the inspection" (§1822.54).
  • Without a warrant and outside an emergency, you can decline entry (Camara). Saying no is what the warrant application has to report: "consent to inspect has been sought and refused" (§1822.51), and it entitles you to 24 hours' notice before a warrant is executed unless a judge finds otherwise (§1822.56).
  • If they have a warrant, read its limits and don't obstruct it (§1822.57). Note the time, the names and what was inspected.

Sources: Verified50,43,47,52,53

Common misconceptions

  • "Code inspectors don't need warrants because it isn't a criminal case." Camara held administrative inspections are searches that need a warrant absent consent or an emergency.
  • "A warrant means they found a violation." An inspection warrant can rest on routine or area inspection standards (§1822.52).
  • "Refusing a warrant is just another refusal." With a warrant, refusal is a misdemeanor (§1822.57).

Sources: Verified43,49,53

How do I get city records?

What the law says

Ask for them under the California Public Records Act (CPRA), Gov. Code §7920.000 and following. The right is constitutional: "The people have the right of access to information concerning the conduct of the people's business, and, therefore, ... the writings of public officials and agencies shall be open to public scrutiny," and "each local agency is hereby required to comply with the California Public Records Act" (Art. I, §3(b)(1), (7)). Laws that further access "shall be broadly construed," and laws that limit it "narrowly construed" (§3(b)(2)).

"Public records" is broad: "any writing containing information relating to the conduct of the public's business prepared, owned, used, or retained by any state or local agency regardless of physical form or characteristics" (Gov. Code §7920.530(a)). That includes emails, texts, spreadsheets, photos and recordings. The California Supreme Court held "that when a city employee uses a personal account to communicate about the conduct of public business, the writings may be subject to disclosure" (City of San Jose v. Superior Court, 2017).

You don't have to explain why: the Act "does not allow limitations on access to a public record based upon the purpose for which the record is being requested" (Gov. Code §7921.300).

Sources: Verified62,8,63,64,65

How a request works, step by step

  • 1. Describe the records. The test is whether your request "reasonably describes an identifiable record or records" (Gov. Code §7922.530(a)). Name the subject, the people, the date range and the kind of record (for example, "emails between the city manager and the city attorney about item 18, Oct 1 to Oct 21, 2026").
  • 2. The city must help you. It must, to the extent reasonable, help you "identify records and information that are responsive," "describe the information technology and physical location in which the records exist," and "provide suggestions for overcoming any practical basis for denying access" (Gov. Code §7922.600(a)).
  • 3. The 10-day answer. Within 10 days of receiving the request, the city must decide whether it seeks disclosable records, "promptly notify" you of the decision "and the reasons therefor," and, if records will be released, "state the estimated date and time when the records will be made available" (Gov. Code §7922.535(a)).
  • 4. Extensions. In "unusual circumstances" (records at other sites, a large volume, consulting another agency, compiling data, a cyberattack, or a declared state of emergency) the city may extend by written notice giving the reasons, for no more than 14 days (§7922.535(b)-(c)).
  • 5. Delivery. Records must be made "promptly available" on payment of "fees covering direct costs of duplication" (§7922.530(a)). Nothing in the Act lets the city "delay or obstruct the inspection or copying of public records" (Gov. Code §7922.500).

Sources: Verified66,67,12,68,69

Costs, formats and inspecting in person

  • Inspecting is free. "Public records are open to inspection at all times during the office hours" of the agency (Gov. Code §7922.525(a)). When you inspect on the premises, you may use your own camera or phone to copy records without being charged, as long as it doesn't touch or damage the record (§7922.530(b)).
  • Electronic records. If the record exists electronically, the city must provide it electronically on request, in any format in which it holds the information (Gov. Code §7922.570). The cost is limited to "the direct cost of producing a copy," except that you pay programming and compilation costs when the request requires "data compilation, extraction, or programming" (Gov. Code §7922.575).
  • Partial release. "Any reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be available ... after deletion of the portions that are exempted by law" (§7922.525(b)). A record with one exempt line should come back with that line blacked out, not withheld whole.

Sources: Verified70,66,71,72

Denials and exemptions

  • A denial of a written request, in whole or in part, "shall be in writing" and must give "the names and titles or positions of each person responsible for the denial" (Gov. Code §7922.540).
  • The city must justify withholding by showing the record is exempt under an express provision, or that "on the facts of the particular case the public interest served by not disclosing the record clearly outweighs the public interest served by disclosure" (Gov. Code §7922.000). The burden is on the city, not on you.
  • If the city discloses an otherwise exempt record to a member of the public, that disclosure waives most of the CPRA's exemptions for that record (Gov. Code §7921.505(b)); the section lists exceptions, such as disclosures in court proceedings or to another agency that agrees to keep it confidential (§7921.505(c)).

Sources: Verified73,74,75

  • Go to court. "Any person may institute a proceeding for injunctive or declarative relief, or for a writ of mandate ... to enforce that person's right" (Gov. Code §7923.000). The petition goes to "the superior court of the county where the records or some part thereof are situated" (Gov. Code §7923.100); for Williams records that is the Colusa County Superior Court. If the refusal isn't justified, "the court shall order the public official to make the record public" (Gov. Code §7923.110(a)).
  • Fees. "If the requester prevails ... the court shall award court costs and reasonable attorney's fees to the requester," paid by the agency. A "clearly frivolous" case can mean paying the agency's fees (Gov. Code §7923.115).

Can the council meet in private?

What the law says

Only for specific reasons the law lists. The starting rule: "All meetings of the legislative body of a local agency shall be open and public, and all persons shall be permitted to attend any meeting" (Gov. Code §54953(a)). A closed session is allowed only where the Brown Act or a few other laws "expressly" authorize it (Gov. Code §54962). The California Constitution backs this: "the meetings of public bodies ... shall be open to public scrutiny," and limits on that access are "narrowly construed" (Art. I, §3(b)(1)-(2)).

A "meeting" isn't only a formal session. It is "any congregation of a majority of the members" to "hear, discuss, deliberate, or take action" on city business (Gov. Code §54952.2(a)). A majority also can't use "a series of communications of any kind, directly or through intermediaries" outside a meeting to discuss or decide city business (§54952.2(b)(1)), which covers chains of calls, emails or texts. On social media, a member may not respond directly to another member's post about city business (§54952.2(b)(3)). With five council members in Williams, three is a majority.

Sources: Verified82,27,8,83,84

The allowed reasons

The main closed-session grounds for a city council:

  • Real estate. To instruct its negotiators on "the price and terms of payment" for buying, selling, exchanging or leasing a specific property (Gov. Code §54956.8).
  • Litigation. To confer with its lawyers about pending or anticipated litigation (Gov. Code §54956.9).
  • Personnel. To consider "the appointment, employment, evaluation of performance, discipline, or dismissal of a public employee," or threats to public services or facilities (Gov. Code §54957).
  • Labor negotiations. To meet with its designated representatives about salaries, benefits and other terms of employment (Gov. Code §54957.6).

Discussing a policy, a budget, a fee or a contract that doesn't fall into one of these categories must happen in open session (§54962).

Sources: Verified85,86,87,88,27

What has to happen before, during and after

  • Before. Closed-session items must be on the posted agenda with a brief description, posted 72 hours ahead (Gov. Code §54954.2(a)(1)). The law gives model wording, such as "CONFERENCE WITH LABOR NEGOTIATORS" with the agency's designated representatives named; using it is a safe harbor (Gov. Code §54954.5). In open session, before going in, the council must disclose the items, and "may consider only those matters covered in its statement" (Gov. Code §54957.7(a)). Public comment is allowed on closed-session items before the council goes in, because they are agenda items (§54954.3(a)(1)).
  • During. A clerk or other designated officer may keep a minute book, which is confidential and available only to council members or, if a violation is alleged, to a court (Gov. Code §54957.2(a)).
  • After. The council must reconvene in open session before adjourning and report any action required by §54957.1 (§54957.7(b)). It must "publicly report any action taken in closed session and the vote or abstention on that action of every member present": real estate agreements once final, approval to sue or defend, settlements once final, actions on claims, actions to "appoint, employ, dismiss, accept the resignation of, or otherwise affect the employment status of a public employee" (identifying the title of the position), and labor agreements once ratified (Gov. Code §54957.1(a)). Copies of contracts, settlements or other documents finally approved in closed session go to people who asked in writing within 24 hours of the agenda posting, or have a standing request, and are present when the session ends; otherwise they are available the next business day (§54957.1(b)-(c)).
  • Pay for top staff. Before final action on the salary or benefits of a local agency executive, the council must orally report a summary of the recommendation in the open meeting where it takes final action (Gov. Code §54953(d)(3)), and it can't hold a special meeting on those executives' pay, except for the budget (Gov. Code §54956(b)).

If you think the rules were broken

  • Void the action: cure and correct. If the council took action in violation of §§54953, 54954.2, 54954.5, 54954.6, 54956 or 54956.5 (for example, a decision made in a closed session that wasn't allowed, or on an item not properly described), first send the council a written demand to "cure or correct" it, clearly describing the action and the problem. The deadline is 90 days after the action, or 30 days if the problem is that it was taken in open session on an item not on the posted agenda under §54954.2. The council has 30 days to cure or correct it or decide not to; if it doesn't act, that counts as a refusal, and you then have 15 days to sue (Gov. Code §54960.1(b)-(c)). Some actions can't be voided, including actions taken in substantial compliance, contracts relied on in good faith, bonds and tax collection (§54960.1(d)).
  • Stop a practice: cease and desist. For a pattern or past practice, send a cease-and-desist letter to the City Clerk by mail or fax within nine months. The council has 30 days to respond with an "unconditional commitment" to stop, made in open session using wording set in the statute; if it doesn't, you have 60 days to sue (Gov. Code §54960.2).
  • In court. A court can stop or prevent violations, and may order the council to audio-record its closed sessions (Gov. Code §54960(a)-(b)). A winning plaintiff may recover costs and attorney fees (§54960.5).
  • Crimes and complaints. A member who attends a meeting where action is taken in violation of the Brown Act, "where the member intends to deprive the public of information to which the member knows or has reason to know the public is entitled," commits a misdemeanor (Gov. Code §54959). Anyone can complain to the District Attorney or the Grand Jury. A participant can't be punished for confidential complaints to them about a perceived violation, or for expressing an opinion about whether a closed-session action was legal (Gov. Code §54963(e)).

Can the city shut off my water?

What the law says

Only after following the Water Shutoff Protection Act, Health and Safety Code §§116900-116926. Since August 1, 2024 it covers community water systems of every size (§116904(d)). A system with more than 200 service connections has had to follow it since April 1, 2020 or earlier (§§116902(e), 116904(a)-(c)). The state lists the Williams system with 1,426 residential and 139 commercial connections.

The core rule: the city "shall not discontinue residential service for nonpayment until a payment by a customer has been delinquent for at least 60 days" (§116908(a)(1)(A)). The act covers homes, apartments, mobilehomes and farmworker housing (§116902(d)). It doesn't cover a shut-off "due to an unauthorized action of a customer" (§116926).

Every covered system must have a written shut-off policy, posted on its website, that includes a payment plan "available for any customer," alternative payment schedules, "a formal mechanism for a customer to contest or appeal a bill," and a phone number to call (§116906(a)-(b)). Where the act and older rules overlap, the act controls, and it doesn't reduce protections that existed at the end of 2018 (§116924).

Sources: Verified95,96,97,98,99,100,101,102

How it works, step by step

  • 60 days late. No residential shut-off for nonpayment before a payment is at least 60 days delinquent (§116908(a)(1)(A)).
  • Notice at least 7 business days before. The city must contact the customer by phone or written notice "no fewer than seven business days before" the shut-off. A written notice must give the amount owed, the date to pay or make arrangements, how to ask for more time, how to appeal the bill, and how to ask for a payment plan. If the bill goes to a different address, a copy goes to the home addressed to "Occupant" (§116908(a)(1)(A)-(C)).
  • A visit if they can't reach you. If the phone and mail both fail, the city must make "a good faith effort to visit the residence" and leave a notice and the policy (§116908(a)(2)).
  • No shut-off during an appeal. If an adult at the home appeals the bill, the city "shall not discontinue residential service while the appeal is pending" (§116908(b)).
  • Medical and income protection. No shut-off if all three are true: a primary care provider certifies a shut-off would be life threatening or a serious health threat to someone in the home; the customer can't pay within the billing cycle (anyone in the household gets CalWORKs, CalFresh, general assistance, Medi-Cal, SSI/SSP or WIC, or the customer declares household income under 200% of the federal poverty level); and the customer agrees to a payment plan (§116910(a)).
  • The plan the city must offer then. One or more of: spreading the balance out, an alternative schedule, a partial or full reduction, or a temporary deferral. Repayment should ordinarily finish within 12 months. Service can be cut only 5 business days after a final notice is posted at the home, and only if the customer misses the plan or current bills for 60 days or more (§116910(b)).
  • Tenants. If the landlord is the customer, the city must make a good-faith effort to tell residents in writing at least 10 days before a shut-off (7 days for a detached single-family home) and that they can become customers "without being required to pay any amount that may be due on the delinquent account." A tenant who becomes the customer may deduct those water payments from rent when rent includes water (§116916(b), (e), (f)). Separately, a landlord may not cut a tenant's utilities to force them out (Civ. Code §789.3).
  • Notices in your language. Required notices must be in English, the languages listed in Civil Code §1632, and any language spoken by 10% or more of customers (§116922).

Sources: Verified99,103,104,105,106

Getting service back

  • Information. After a shut-off, the city must tell the customer "how to restore residential service" (§116912).
  • Reconnection fee caps for lower-income households. For a household under 200% of the federal poverty level (shown the same way as above), the reconnection fee can't exceed $50 during normal hours or $150 after hours, or the actual cost if lower, adjusted each year for inflation since 2021. Interest on a late bill must be waived once every 12 months (§116914).
  • Reporting. Each covered system must post its yearly number of shut-offs for inability to pay on its website and report it to the State Water Board (§116918).

Sources: Verified107,108,109

How do I fight a city fine, citation or lien?

What the law says

State law lets a city "by ordinance make any violation of any ordinance enacted by the local agency subject to an administrative fine or penalty," and requires the ordinance to set out "the administrative procedures that shall govern the imposition, enforcement, collection, and administrative review" of those fines (Gov. Code §53069.4(a)(1)). Where the violation would otherwise be an infraction, the fine can't exceed the infraction limits in Gov. Code §§25132 and 36900(b) (§53069.4(a)(1)).

For building, plumbing, electrical, structural or zoning problems that are not an immediate danger, the ordinance must give "a reasonable period of time" to fix a continuing violation before fines start (§53069.4(a)(2)(A)).

Fines also have constitutional limits. Both constitutions forbid excessive fines, and the federal clause binds cities (Timbs v. Indiana, 2019). California courts weigh culpability, the link between harm and penalty, penalties in similar laws, and "the defendant's ability to pay" (People ex rel. Lockyer v. R.J. Reynolds, 2005). A fine "imposed ... as a result of a violation of law" isn't a tax (Art. XIII C, §1(e)(5)).

Sources: Verified60,114,8,115

How a citation works, step by step

Williams adopted its "administrative citations ordinance" under §53069.4 (Williams Municipal Code §8.16.460). The steps and deadlines:

  • What the citation must say. The date, place, code section and violation; the fine; a correction date for non-emergency health or safety violations; how and where to pay; how to contest and where to get a hearing request form; and how to ask for a deposit waiver (§8.16.480(B)).
  • Service. In person if possible; otherwise by mail or posting. Mailed service counts three days after mailing (§§8.16.485, 8.16.487).
  • Fix it in time and there's no fine. If a non-emergency health or safety violation is corrected before the correction date, "no fine shall be imposed." Otherwise the fine is due within 21 calendar days of the correction date (§8.16.500(A)).
  • Ask for a hearing within 21 days. Return a request for hearing form to the City Clerk within 21 days from the date of the citation, with either the fine as a deposit or an approved deposit waiver (§8.16.510(A)-(B)).
  • Can't afford the deposit? File a deposit waiver request with the finance officer within 10 days of the citation, with a sworn statement and documents showing you can't pay in advance. The answer comes within two business days, in writing with reasons (§8.16.520).
  • The hearing. Set 15 to 60 days after your request, with at least 10 days' notice. The city must prove the violation and that you're responsible "by a preponderance of the evidence." You can cross-examine the officer, present evidence and bring a lawyer. Witnesses testify under oath (§§8.16.510(C), 8.16.530). A hearing officer who is a city employee can't have had any responsibility for investigating or enforcing the case (§8.16.475(C)).
  • The decision. A written decision with reasons. If the citation is canceled or reduced, a deposit is refunded with interest. If it's upheld and you had a waiver, the decision sets a payment schedule (§8.16.540).
  • Late fines grow. A late fine adds a 10% penalty, then interest at 10% a year (§8.16.550). Not showing up at your hearing forfeits the fine and counts as a "failure to exhaust administrative remedies," which can cost you a court appeal (§8.16.570(A)).

Sources: Verified59

Going to court

  • Citation fines. Within 20 days after the final decision is served, you can file an appeal in superior court, "where the same shall be heard de novo" (heard fresh). It is a limited civil case, and you must serve a copy of the notice of appeal on the city in person or by first-class mail (Gov. Code §53069.4(b)(1)). If no appeal is filed in time, the decision "shall be deemed confirmed" (§53069.4(c)). Williams is in Colusa County, so the superior court is the Colusa County Superior Court. It has two locations in Colusa (547 Market St. and 532 Oak St.); call (530) 458-5149 to ask where to file.
  • Other hearing-officer decisions, such as a nuisance decision, are reviewed by a petition for writ of mandate under Code of Civil Procedure §1094.6 (Williams Municipal Code §8.16.590(A)). That petition must be filed "not later than the 90th day following the date on which the decision becomes final" (CCP §1094.6(b)).
  • Get help early. Court deadlines are short and strict. A lawyer can tell you which one applies to your paper; the court's website is the place to start.

Sources: Verified60,61,116,117

Liens and charges on the tax bill

  • Unpaid citation fines. They are civil debts, and where the violation relates to the person's own real property, "a lien may be filed by the city against such real property" (§8.16.560).
  • Nuisance abatement costs. If the city fixes a nuisance itself, the cost becomes the owner's personal obligation and can be collected as a lien or special assessment (§8.16.140), with administrative costs and 10% yearly interest (§8.16.160). State law allows both methods (Gov. Code §§38773.1, 38773.5).
  • Lien steps. The city keeps an itemized cost report and may invoice the owner; if the invoice isn't paid within 30 days, it may move to a lien or assessment (§8.16.160(D)). Notice of the lien is served like a court summons, a council hearing is held at least 10 days later, and if the council confirms, the lien is recorded and has "the force, effect and priority of a judgment lien." It can be foreclosed (§8.16.170). The state rules are the same (Gov. Code §38773.1(b)-(c)).
  • Special assessment steps. Notice goes by certified mail, a council hearing is held at least 10 days later, and the confirmed amount is collected with property taxes. "The property may be sold after three years by the tax collector for unpaid delinquent assessments" (§8.16.180). State law requires the notice to say so (Gov. Code §38773.5(c)).
  • Garbage and water bills. Unpaid garbage charges may be recorded "as a special assessment and lien" and collected with property taxes (§8.08.220). The council acted on delinquent Recology garbage accounts in Resolution 25-09 (June 18, 2025) and placed delinquent Recology charges on the property tax roll in Resolution 26-17 (June 17, 2026). Unpaid water charges can also become a lien (§13.04.221(E)). See the water shut-off answer above.

How to contest before a lien attaches

  • Answer the first notice. Can the city raise taxes or fees without a vote?

What the Constitution says

Not taxes. Every local tax is either general or special (Art. XIII C, §2(a)). "No local government may impose, extend, or increase any general tax unless and until that tax is submitted to the electorate and approved by a majority vote" (§2(b)), and a special tax needs "a two-thirds vote" (§2(d)). State law says the same for general taxes (Gov. Code §53723).

  • General tax: "any tax imposed for general governmental purposes" (§1(a)).
  • Special tax: "any tax imposed for specific purposes, including a tax imposed for specific purposes, which is placed into a general fund" (§1(d)).

Fees and assessments follow different rules, set out below. Proposition 13 (Art. XIII A) also caps the general property tax at "One percent (1%) of the full cash value," collected by the county (§1(a)), and lets cities impose special taxes only "by a two-thirds vote of the qualified electors" (§4).

Sources: Verified8,125

Tax or fee? How it works

Since Proposition 26 (2010), "tax" means "any levy, charge, or exaction of any kind imposed by a local government," except seven kinds of charges (Art. XIII C, §1(e)):

  • A charge for a specific benefit or privilege, or a specific service or product, given directly to the payer and not to others, that doesn't exceed the reasonable cost (§1(e)(1)-(2)).
  • Reasonable regulatory costs for licenses, permits, inspections and audits (§1(e)(3)).
  • A charge to enter, use, buy, rent or lease local government property (§1(e)(4)).
  • A fine or penalty "as a result of a violation of law" (§1(e)(5)).
  • A charge imposed as a condition of property development (§1(e)(6)).
  • Assessments and property-related fees that follow Article XIII D (§1(e)(7)).

The city has to prove a charge is not a tax: "The local government bears the burden of proving by a preponderance of the evidence that a levy, charge, or other exaction is not a tax, that the amount is no more than necessary to cover the reasonable costs" and that it is fairly allocated (§1(e)). A charge that fits none of the seven exceptions is a tax and needs a vote.

Sources: Verified8

Property-related fees and assessments

  • What's covered. No tax, assessment, fee or charge may be imposed "upon any parcel of property or upon any person as an incident of property ownership" except the 1% property tax, a special tax approved by two-thirds, assessments, and property-related fees, each under its own rules (Art. XIII D, §3(a)).
  • Water, sewer and trash fees. These go through a notice, a hearing at least 45 days later, and a majority written protest that can stop the increase, but no election (§6(a), (c)). The water rate answer above walks through that protest. Other property-related fees need a vote of the affected owners or two-thirds of the voters in the area (§6(c)).
  • Assessments. An assessment may charge only for a "special benefit," and no more than each parcel's proportional share, backed by "a detailed engineer's report" (§4(a)-(b)). Each owner gets a mailed notice with a ballot, and the hearing is held at least 45 days later. "The agency shall not impose an assessment if there is a majority protest," meaning the ballots against outweigh those in favor, "weighted according to the proportional financial obligation of the affected property" (§4(c)-(e)).
  • Voters can cut them. The initiative power "shall not be prohibited or otherwise limited in matters of reducing or repealing any local tax, assessment, fee or charge" (Art. XIII C, §3).

If the city doesn't follow the rules

  • Speak and protest on the record. Comment at the hearing, and for a §6 fee or a §4 assessment, file your written protest or ballot before the hearing closes.
  • The burden is on the city. In a court challenge, the city must prove a charge isn't a tax (Art. XIII C, §1(e)), that an assessment's parcels get a special benefit (Art.   • Use the initiative. Voters may propose reducing or repealing a local tax, fee or assessment (Art. XIII C, §3). See initiative and referendum.

Common misconceptions

  • "Calling it a fee gets around the vote." The label doesn't matter; unless the charge fits one of the seven exceptions, it is a tax, and the city must prove it isn't (Art. XIII C, §1(e)).

Can officials retaliate if I criticize them?

What the law says

No. Criticizing government is protected speech under the First Amendment, which binds cities through the Fourteenth Amendment (Gitlow v. New York, 1925). California's Constitution adds: "Every person may freely speak, write and publish his or her sentiments on all subjects, being responsible for the abuse of this right" (Art. I, §2(a)), and "The people have the right to instruct their representatives, petition government for redress of grievances, and assemble freely" (Art. I, §3(a)).

Debate about officials "may well include vehement, caustic, and sometimes unpleasantly sharp attacks on government and public officials," and an official suing for defamation must prove "actual malice" (New York Times Co. v. Sullivan, 1964). Officials can't punish someone for protected speech.

At meetings, the Brown Act says a council "shall not prohibit public criticism of the policies, procedures, programs, or services of the agency, or of the acts or omissions of the legislative body" (Gov. Code §54954.3(c)).

Sources: Verified134,135,8,136,137,138

What officials can and can't do

  • Can: set "reasonable regulations," including time limits for each speaker and for a topic (§54954.3(b)(1)). When they limit time, a speaker using a translator gets at least twice the time, unless simultaneous translation is used (§54954.3(b)(2)-(3)).
  • Can: clear the room if a meeting is "willfully interrupted" so that order can't be restored by removing the people disrupting it. Only agenda items may then be taken up, and the press may stay (Gov. Code §54957.9). Willfully disturbing or breaking up a lawful meeting is a misdemeanor (Penal Code §403).
  • Can't: prohibit criticism of the city's policies, services or the council's acts (§54954.3(c)), or punish you for protected speech.
  • Social media. An official's post is government action only if the official "possessed actual authority to speak on the State's behalf on a particular matter" and "purported to exercise that authority" in the posts (Lindke v. Freed, 2024). That test decides whether blocking someone on a personal page can be challenged.
  • City employees. An employee speaking as a citizen on matters of public concern is protected, balanced against the employer's interests; speech made as part of the job is not (Pickering, 1968; Garcetti, 2006).

Sources: Verified138,139,140,137,38,141,142,143

How retaliation claims work

  • Federal civil rights law. 42 U.S.C. §1983 makes "every person who, under color of any statute, ordinance, regulation, custom, or usage, of any State" deprives someone of constitutional rights "liable to the party injured". A city itself can be sued when an official policy or custom causes the violation, not merely because it employs the wrongdoer (Monell).
  • Arrests. Probable cause usually defeats a retaliatory-arrest claim, with an exception where there is "objective evidence" that similarly situated people who weren't speaking out weren't arrested (Nieves v. Bartlett, 2019). That evidence doesn't have to be a precise comparison (Gonzalez v. Trevino, 2024). Lozman v. Riviera Beach (2018) let a man arrested at a city council meeting pursue a claim that the city had an official policy of retaliating against him.
  • Lawsuits meant to silence you (anti-SLAPP). If someone sues you over a statement made at a council meeting, about an issue the council is considering, or in a public forum on an issue of public interest, you can file a "special motion to strike" (CCP §425.16(b)(1), (e)). The suit is dismissed unless the plaintiff shows "a probability that the plaintiff will prevail." The motion is due within 60 days of service, discovery is paused, and a winning defendant recovers attorney's fees (§425.16(c)(1), (f), (g)).
  • State-law damage claims against the city generally must first be presented as a written claim, within six months for injury to a person or personal property and one year for others (Gov. Code §911.2), with a lawsuit due six months after a written rejection (Gov. Code §945.6(a)(1)). The U.S. Supreme Court has held that a state notice-of-claim rule cannot be applied to a federal §1983 suit brought in state court (Felder v. Casey, 487 U.S. 131 (1988)).

Sources: Verified144,145,137,146,38,147,148,149,150

Common misconceptions

  • "The council can stop me from criticizing a member by name." It can't prohibit criticism of the council's acts or the city's policies (§54954.3(c)), though it can enforce neutral time limits (§54954.3(b)(1)).

  • "If police had probable cause, there can't be a retaliation claim." Usually true, but Nieves and Gonzalez recognize an exception.
